# Chapter 03. Latent Dynamics Models

## 03.01. Why Latent Dynamics

![](images/image1.png){width="7.268055555555556in" height="7.268055555555556in"}

Latent dynamics models address a central difficulty in Physical AI: the raw sensory world is far too detailed to predict directly at every moment. Cameras produce millions of pixels, LiDAR generates large point clouds, and proprioceptive sensors continuously report joint, velocity, force, and inertial measurements. Predicting every observation value is computationally expensive and often unnecessary for intelligent action.

Instead of modeling future observations directly, latent dynamics models transform observations into compact internal states that preserve information important for understanding how the environment evolves. An encoder maps high-dimensional sensory inputs into a latent representation, and a dynamics model predicts how this representation changes over time. The resulting internal trajectory can describe future world states without reconstructing every sensory detail.

This compression is particularly important because much of an observation is irrelevant to the agent\'s immediate objectives. A mobile robot navigating a corridor does not need to predict the exact texture of every wall or subtle variations in illumination. It needs representations of obstacles, free space, motion, geometry, traversability, and other factors that influence future actions. Latent states can concentrate computational capacity on these task-relevant structures.

Latent dynamics therefore separate physical significance from observational complexity. Two camera images may differ substantially at the pixel level because of shadows, reflections, exposure, or viewpoint changes while describing nearly the same physical situation. A useful latent representation can map such observations to nearby internal states. The dynamics model then learns transitions between meaningful states rather than wasting capacity predicting superficial visual variation.

This principle becomes even more valuable in multimodal Physical AI systems. Cameras, LiDAR, radar, IMUs, GNSS, force sensors, and proprioception describe the same environment through fundamentally different measurement spaces. Predicting each modality independently can create fragmented representations. A shared or coordinated latent state can instead integrate complementary evidence into a compact description of the underlying physical situation.

Temporal prediction also becomes more manageable in latent space. Direct pixel prediction must represent countless possible low-level variations, many of which have little consequence for planning. Latent prediction can emphasize persistent structure such as object identity, relative position, velocity, contact relationships, scene geometry, and agent state. The model effectively asks what important properties of the world will change rather than what every sensor value will look like.

Latent dynamics are especially useful under partial observability. A robot rarely observes the complete physical state from a single sensor frame because objects can be occluded, sensors have limited range, and important variables may not be directly measurable. By integrating observations across time, the latent state can function as an internal belief-like representation containing information inferred from previous observations as well as information visible now.

Another major advantage is computational efficiency. Physical AI systems frequently operate under strict latency, power, memory, and thermal constraints, particularly when models execute on edge computers embedded in robots or vehicles. Reducing high-dimensional observations to smaller latent vectors or feature maps decreases the amount of information that must pass through temporal prediction modules. More model capacity can consequently be devoted to reasoning about dynamics and longer horizons.

The advantage becomes increasingly important during multi-step prediction. A world model may need to imagine dozens or hundreds of future transitions before selecting an action. Repeatedly generating full-resolution images or sensor observations during every hypothetical rollout can become prohibitively expensive. Latent dynamics allow the model to roll the internal state forward repeatedly and decode observations only when explicit reconstruction or visualization is actually required.

This creates an important distinction between prediction for understanding and prediction for rendering. A robot generally does not need photorealistic future images to determine whether a corridor will remain traversable or whether another agent may intersect its trajectory. It needs predictive variables sufficient for decision making. Latent dynamics permit the internal model to represent such variables implicitly without requiring every future state to become a complete synthetic observation.

Latent prediction also supports action-conditioned reasoning. The future of a physical system depends not only on its present state but also on what the agent does. Steering, accelerating, braking, moving a manipulator, or applying force changes subsequent states. A latent dynamics function can therefore receive the current latent state together with an action and estimate the resulting next state, providing a compact learned transition mechanism for imagined interaction.

Once such a transition mechanism exists, the robot can evaluate actions internally before executing them physically. Multiple candidate action sequences can be rolled forward through latent space, producing alternative predicted trajectories. A planner can compare these trajectories according to safety, progress, energy consumption, collision risk, reward, or other objectives. Latent dynamics thus provide a computational foundation for imagination-based planning and model-based reinforcement learning.

The latent state should not be understood simply as conventional dimensionality reduction. An effective representation must retain information required to predict future consequences while discarding details that do not matter. This creates a representation-learning problem in which compression, predictability, controllability, semantics, geometry, and temporal persistence may all influence what the internal state eventually encodes.

Too much compression can remove variables necessary for accurate dynamics, while too little compression recreates the complexity of observation-space modeling. The desired latent representation therefore lies between these extremes. It should be sufficiently compact for efficient prediction but sufficiently expressive to preserve distinctions that matter for future evolution, action consequences, uncertainty, and planning.

Stochasticity further strengthens the motivation for latent modeling. Physical environments often contain futures that cannot be represented adequately by a single deterministic trajectory because humans, vehicles, movable objects, and environmental processes may behave in several plausible ways. Stochastic latent dynamics can represent distributions over future states, allowing the world model to preserve multiple hypotheses without generating every possible future observation explicitly.

Latent dynamics also create a natural bridge between perception and control. Perception converts sensory streams into an internal state, dynamics predicts how that state may evolve, and planning searches through predicted state trajectories to choose actions. Control then applies the selected action to the physical system, producing new observations that update the latent state. The world model becomes part of a continuous perception-prediction-action loop rather than an isolated forecasting component.

For Physical AI, this loop is fundamental because intelligence must operate under real-world time constraints while dealing with enormous sensory complexity. Latent dynamics provide a practical abstraction layer between raw measurements and physical reasoning. By learning what should be preserved, what can be ignored, and how meaningful internal states change, they enable scalable prediction, efficient imagination, longer-horizon planning, and ultimately more capable interaction with the physical world.

## 03.02. Encoder Dynamics Decoder Architecture

![](images/image2.png){width="7.268055555555556in" height="7.268055555555556in"}

The encoder-dynamics-decoder architecture provides a structured way for a world model to transform complex observations into compact internal states, predict how those states evolve, and optionally reconstruct meaningful observations. Instead of forcing one network to perform perception, temporal reasoning, and generation simultaneously, the architecture separates these functions into components that can be optimized while remaining connected through a shared latent representation.

The encoder forms the perceptual entrance to the model. At time (t), an observation (o_t) may contain camera images, LiDAR measurements, radar returns, proprioception, IMU signals, or other sensory information. The encoder (E_\\phi) transforms these high-dimensional measurements into a latent state (z_t = E_\\phi(o_t)), reducing observational complexity while attempting to preserve information required for future prediction and interaction.

Encoding is more than simple data compression. A useful latent state must retain physical and semantic properties that influence future evolution, such as object locations, velocities, geometry, contact conditions, agent configuration, traversability, and environmental context. At the same time, it should suppress irrelevant variation such as image noise, minor illumination changes, redundant pixels, or sensor-specific artifacts that contribute little to prediction or decision making.

For multimodal Physical AI, different sensor streams can be processed by modality-specific encoders before their features are fused into a common latent representation. Cameras may contribute appearance and semantic information, LiDAR may provide geometry and depth, radar may capture motion under difficult visibility, and proprioception may describe the robot itself. The resulting latent state can therefore represent the environment and embodied agent within a unified internal coordinate system.

The dynamics component operates on this latent representation rather than directly on raw observations. Its fundamental task is to estimate how the internal state changes over time. In its simplest form, the transition can be expressed as (z_{t+1}=F_\\theta(z_t)). For an embodied agent whose behavior changes the environment, the more useful formulation is action-conditioned dynamics, (z_{t+1}=F_\\theta(z_t,a_t)), where (a_t) represents the action executed at the current state.

This action conditioning distinguishes a passive predictive model from an interactive world model. A robot does not merely observe how the environment changes independently; its steering commands, wheel velocities, joint motions, forces, grasps, and other actions influence subsequent physical states. By learning these relationships in latent space, the dynamics model can predict not only what may happen next but also what may happen if the agent performs a particular action.

The transition model can take many architectural forms depending on the temporal structure and application. Recurrent networks can maintain compact sequential state, state-space models can explicitly represent evolving hidden variables, and Transformer-based models can capture dependencies across longer temporal contexts. Regardless of implementation, the central objective remains the same: preserve sufficient temporal structure so that predicted latent states remain meaningful representations of plausible future physical situations.

Multi-step prediction is obtained by repeatedly applying the dynamics model. Starting from (z_t), the model predicts (z_{t+1}), then (z_{t+2}), and continues toward a desired horizon. When actions are included, a candidate sequence (a_t,a_{t+1},\...,a_{t+H-1}) produces a corresponding imagined latent trajectory. This makes the dynamics module an internal simulator capable of evaluating possible futures without physically executing every candidate behavior.

The decoder performs the complementary mapping from latent space back toward an observation or another interpretable output. A decoder (D_\\psi) may produce (\\hat{o}\*t=D\*\\psi(z_t)), reconstructing images, depth, occupancy, semantic maps, sensor features, or other representations. Reconstruction can encourage the latent state to preserve useful information and provides a direct mechanism for inspecting what the internal model has retained or predicted.

However, the decoder does not always need to reconstruct every raw sensor measurement. For planning and control, generating complete photorealistic images may add substantial computation without improving action selection. A decoder can instead produce task-relevant quantities such as future occupancy, object motion, collision probability, traversability, reward, or value. Some latent predictive architectures may even operate primarily in representation space and use little or no explicit observation decoder.

The three components consequently establish a transformation chain from observation to representation, representation to future representation, and representation back to interpretable output. Conceptually, this can be written as (o_t \\rightarrow E_\\phi \\rightarrow z_t \\rightarrow F_\\theta \\rightarrow z_{t+1} \\rightarrow D_\\psi \\rightarrow \\hat{o}_{t+1}). The intermediate latent state separates perceptual representation from temporal prediction and allows each stage to specialize in a different computational problem.

Training can connect these components through several complementary objectives. Reconstruction losses encourage the decoder to recover information from encoded states, while prediction losses require predicted latent states or decoded observations to agree with future data. Representation objectives can enforce temporal consistency, invariance, or separation between meaningful states. The exact balance determines whether the learned latent space emphasizes visual fidelity, dynamics, semantics, control relevance, or combinations of these properties.

A major design issue is preventing the encoder and decoder from dominating the dynamics objective. If reconstruction accuracy receives excessive emphasis, the latent representation may preserve fine sensory details that are expensive to predict but irrelevant to physical reasoning. Conversely, excessive compression can discard variables needed to model future transitions. Successful architectures therefore seek a representation that is compact enough for efficient rollout yet sufficiently informative for prediction and downstream decisions.

Uncertainty can also be incorporated into the architecture. Rather than predicting only one (z_{t+1}), stochastic dynamics can represent a distribution over possible next latent states. This is useful when observations are incomplete or when other agents and environmental processes have multiple plausible futures. The decoder or downstream prediction heads can then translate these alternative latent futures into uncertainty-aware estimates relevant to planning and risk assessment.

The architecture naturally supports closed-loop operation. New observations are encoded into the current latent state, the dynamics model imagines possible future transitions, and a planner evaluates candidate trajectories. After an action is selected and executed, new sensory observations become available and are encoded again. Prediction and observation can therefore continually correct one another, preventing the world model from relying indefinitely on an internally imagined trajectory that may diverge from reality.

For Physical AI, the encoder-dynamics-decoder architecture is valuable because it separates three fundamental questions: what is happening now, what is likely to happen next, and what predicted information should be exposed for interpretation or action. This separation creates a scalable foundation for latent world modeling and leads naturally to recurrent state-space models, stochastic latent dynamics, long-horizon rollouts, action-conditioned prediction, planning, control, and model-based reinforcement learning.

## 03.03. Latent State Representation

![](images/image3.png){width="7.268055555555556in" height="7.268055555555556in"}

A latent state representation is the internal description through which a world model summarizes what it currently believes about the physical environment. Rather than storing every camera pixel, LiDAR point, radar return, or proprioceptive measurement independently, the model transforms observations into a compact state (z_t). This state is intended to retain information needed to understand the present, predict future transitions, and support effective action.

The latent state differs fundamentally from a raw observation. An observation (o_t) describes what sensors measure at a particular moment, whereas (z_t) attempts to represent the underlying situation that generated those measurements. Multiple observations with different lighting, viewpoints, noise patterns, or sensor characteristics may therefore correspond to similar latent states when they describe essentially the same physical configuration.

For Physical AI, a useful latent representation should capture properties that influence interaction with the environment. These may include spatial geometry, object identity, position, velocity, orientation, free space, occupancy, traversability, contact relationships, robot configuration, and task-relevant semantic context. Not every property must appear as an explicitly labeled variable; many can be distributed implicitly across learned features while still influencing prediction and control.

Compactness is one of the central motivations for latent representation. Modern robots may receive millions of sensory values during each perception cycle, making direct temporal reasoning expensive. Mapping these observations into a lower-dimensional representation reduces the computational burden placed on the dynamics model. This enables faster inference, longer predictive rollouts, reduced memory consumption, and more practical deployment on power- and thermal-constrained edge computing platforms.

Compactness alone, however, does not define a good latent state. A representation can be extremely small while discarding information essential for future prediction. The objective is therefore selective compression: irrelevant observational details should be removed while dynamically important variables are retained. The latent state must preserve enough information to distinguish situations that may lead to different future outcomes or require different actions.

Temporal sufficiency is particularly important. If two observations look similar but correspond to objects moving in different directions, their future states may be very different. A latent state derived only from the current frame may fail to distinguish them. Integrating observation history allows the representation to encode motion, temporal trends, hidden variables, and other information required to predict what happens next rather than merely describing what is visible now.

This makes latent state representation closely related to the belief state used in partially observable systems. Physical agents rarely observe the complete world directly because objects become occluded, sensors have finite range, measurements contain noise, and many physical properties cannot be sensed instantaneously. By integrating current observations with memory of previous states, a latent representation can maintain estimates of relevant conditions even when they temporarily disappear from direct observation.

A latent state may be represented in several mathematical forms. A compact vector can summarize the global state of an environment, while spatial feature maps can retain geometric organization. Sets of object-centric tokens can represent entities and their relationships, and structured latent variables can separate geometry, semantics, motion, or agent state. The appropriate representation depends on what information must be predicted and how downstream planning or control will consume it.

Spatial structure is especially important for mobile robots and autonomous systems. Compressing an entire environment into a single unstructured vector may make geometric reasoning difficult. Spatial latent representations such as feature grids, bird\'s-eye-view features, occupancy-oriented features, or learned spatial tokens preserve relationships between locations. This can make future motion, collision, traversability, and interaction easier for subsequent dynamics and planning modules to reason about.

Object-centric latent representations provide another useful abstraction. Instead of treating a scene as one homogeneous feature field, the model can represent relevant entities using separate latent variables or tokens. Each representation may encode attributes such as position, velocity, orientation, category, or interaction state. Dynamics can then model not only individual object motion but also relationships and potential interactions among objects, the robot, and the surrounding environment.

The representation must also describe the embodied agent itself. A world model that encodes only the external environment cannot fully predict the consequences of physical actions. Robot pose, velocity, joint configuration, actuator condition, contact state, payload, and other proprioceptive variables may influence future transitions. The latent state can therefore combine an external world representation with an internal representation of the agent that is acting within that world.

Multimodal sensing introduces the question of whether each modality should maintain its own latent space or contribute to a shared representation. Modality-specific features can preserve information unique to cameras, LiDAR, radar, IMUs, or proprioception, while a shared latent state can capture their common interpretation of the physical world. Hybrid approaches can retain modality-specific features while also constructing fused features for prediction, reasoning, and control.

A robust latent representation should remain relatively invariant to observational changes that do not alter the underlying physical situation. Variations in illumination, minor sensor noise, texture, weather, or viewpoint should not cause unnecessarily large changes in the internal state. At the same time, the representation must remain sensitive to physically important differences such as an obstacle entering the path, a surface becoming unsafe, or another agent changing velocity.

Predictability is another desirable property. A representation designed only to reconstruct the current observation may preserve many details that are difficult or unnecessary to predict. Latent world models instead benefit from states whose future evolution follows learnable regularities. Representation learning and dynamics learning are therefore strongly coupled: the encoder should construct states that the dynamics model can predict, while the dynamics objective encourages the encoder to discover temporally meaningful structure.

Controllability also influences representation quality. Two states may appear perceptually similar yet respond differently to the same action because of friction, contact, payload, terrain, or hidden mechanical conditions. If these differences matter for action outcomes, the latent state should preserve them. A representation useful for Physical AI must consequently describe not only what the world looks like but also those properties that determine how the world responds when the agent acts.

Uncertainty should not necessarily disappear during compression. When observations are ambiguous, forcing the model into one precise latent interpretation can produce overconfident predictions. Probabilistic or stochastic latent representations can maintain uncertainty over hidden conditions or alternative explanations. Future dynamics can then propagate this uncertainty, allowing planning systems to distinguish predictable trajectories from futures where additional caution, sensing, or alternative actions may be required.

The quality of a latent state is ultimately determined by what it enables downstream. A representation is valuable when dynamics can predict it consistently, planners can evaluate future possibilities from it, controllers can select effective actions, and the system remains robust when observations become noisy or incomplete. Reconstruction quality alone is therefore insufficient; prediction accuracy, temporal consistency, control performance, generalization, and planning utility provide more meaningful measures.

Within the latent dynamics framework, the latent state becomes the computational meeting point between perception, memory, prediction, and action. Observations update (z_t), the dynamics model transforms (z_t) into possible future states, and candidate actions alter those predicted transitions. By learning a compact but physically meaningful internal representation, a world model gains a practical state space in which Physical AI can remember, predict, imagine, plan, and interact with the real world.

## 03.04. Recurrent State Space Models

![](images/image4.png){width="7.268055555555556in" height="7.268055555555556in"}

Recurrent State Space Models provide a practical framework for world models that must maintain an internal understanding of a physical environment over time. Instead of treating every observation independently, they recursively update a hidden or latent state using previous internal information, new observations, and often the agent\'s actions. This allows a Physical AI system to construct a temporally continuous representation even when individual sensor measurements are incomplete.

The basic state-space perspective separates an underlying state from the observations generated by that state. At time (t), the agent receives an observation (o_t), while an internal state (z_t) summarizes information believed to be relevant about the world. A transition model predicts how this state evolves, while an observation or representation model connects sensor measurements with the internal state used for prediction.

Recurrence gives this architecture memory. Rather than estimating (z_t) solely from (o_t), the model incorporates information carried from (z_{t-1}). Conceptually, the update can be written as (z_t = F_\\theta(z_{t-1}, a_{t-1}, o_t)). The previous state supplies historical context, the action describes how the agent attempted to change the world, and the new observation provides evidence for correcting the internal estimate.

This structure is especially useful under partial observability. A camera may temporarily lose sight of an object, LiDAR may be occluded, GNSS may degrade, or a physical property may never be directly measurable. A recurrent state can preserve information inferred earlier and combine it with subsequent evidence. The model therefore maintains a belief-like internal state rather than depending entirely on what happens to be visible in the current sensor frame.

Many modern latent world models distinguish deterministic recurrent memory from stochastic latent variables. A deterministic hidden state (h_t) can accumulate temporal information through a recurrent transition, while a stochastic state (z_t) represents uncertain or variable aspects of the current situation. Together, these variables provide both persistent memory and a mechanism for representing uncertainty about physical states that cannot be determined exactly from observations.

A typical recurrent state-space model therefore contains two closely related processes. During observation, an inference or posterior model combines the predicted internal state with the actual observation to estimate the current latent state. During imagination, a prior model predicts future latent states using only the recurrent state and candidate actions. This distinction allows the same world model to learn from reality and later simulate futures without receiving future observations.

The observation phase continuously anchors the internal model to reality. When a new sensor measurement arrives, its encoded representation is combined with the recurrent prediction to update the current state. If the model expected one situation but sensors reveal another, the inferred state can correct the prediction. This repeated prediction-and-correction mechanism helps prevent errors from accumulating indefinitely during normal closed-loop operation.

The imagination phase removes access to future observations. Starting from the current inferred state, the model applies candidate actions and repeatedly predicts subsequent states. A sequence such as (a_t, a_{t+1}, \..., a_{t+H-1}) can generate an imagined trajectory extending from (z_t) toward (z_{t+H}). Planning can then evaluate multiple hypothetical futures without executing every candidate action in the physical environment.

Recurrence makes this rollout computationally attractive because the complete history does not need to be processed again at every step. Relevant historical information has already been summarized within the recurrent state. Each transition updates this compact memory incrementally. For edge-based Physical AI, this can reduce repeated computation and support real-time temporal reasoning over continuous streams from cameras, LiDAR, IMUs, proprioception, and other sensors.

Action conditioning is essential when the model represents an embodied agent. The transition from one state to another depends not only on environmental dynamics but also on motor commands issued by the robot. Wheel velocities change vehicle pose, joint commands alter manipulator configuration, and contact actions modify object relationships. Incorporating (a_t) allows the recurrent state-space model to learn how physical consequences depend on the agent\'s behavior.

The recurrent state should contain information sufficient for future prediction rather than simply memorize observations. This encourages the model to retain persistent variables such as motion, object relationships, geometry, robot configuration, and other dynamically relevant properties. Information that has little influence on future transitions can be compressed away, creating an internal memory organized around prediction and interaction rather than exact reproduction of sensory history.

Training commonly requires consistency between states inferred from observations and states predicted by the dynamics model. The observation-conditioned posterior has access to evidence that the predictive prior does not possess, so it can provide a stronger estimate of the current latent state. Learning encourages the prior to approximate this inferred state using previous state and action information, gradually teaching the dynamics model to predict plausible transitions without seeing the future observation.

Reconstruction or prediction heads can connect recurrent latent states to observable quantities. The model may reconstruct camera features, depth, occupancy, semantic information, proprioception, reward, or other task-relevant signals. However, full sensory reconstruction is not always necessary. For planning-oriented systems, the latent state can instead support specialized heads for collision risk, traversability, value, object motion, or other quantities directly useful for decision making.

Stochastic recurrent state-space models are particularly useful when the future is inherently ambiguous. A pedestrian may choose different paths, an object may move unpredictably, or sensor evidence may support several interpretations. Rather than forcing one deterministic future, stochastic latent variables can represent alternative possibilities. Recurrent memory supplies temporal context while probabilistic latent states preserve uncertainty about which future transition will actually occur.

Long-horizon prediction remains challenging because small model errors can compound through repeated transitions. Recurrent architectures reduce the need to revisit the full observation history, but they do not eliminate model drift. During real-world operation, new observations periodically correct the state. During imagination, uncertainty estimation, regularization, multi-step training, and representations emphasizing predictable structure can help maintain useful trajectories over longer horizons.

Recurrent State Space Models consequently connect several central ideas of latent world modeling. They combine memory with compact state representation, separate observation-based inference from predictive imagination, incorporate actions into temporal dynamics, and support uncertainty when future states are not uniquely determined. These properties make them suitable foundations for model-based reinforcement learning, planning, navigation, manipulation, and autonomous control.

For Physical AI, their deeper importance lies in converting continuous sensor streams into an evolving internal belief about the physical world. The agent does not repeatedly rebuild its understanding from scratch; it carries forward what it has learned, predicts how that knowledge should change, and corrects the prediction when reality provides new evidence. This recurrent cycle of remember, predict, observe, and update provides a computational foundation for persistent world understanding and action over time.

## 03.05. Stochastic Latent Dynamics

![](images/image5.png){width="7.268055555555556in" height="7.268055555555556in"}

Stochastic latent dynamics extend latent world models beyond the assumption that every current state and action lead to one uniquely determined future. Real physical environments contain uncertainty arising from incomplete observations, sensor noise, hidden variables, unpredictable interactions, and genuinely variable behavior. Instead of predicting only one next latent state, a stochastic model represents a distribution of plausible future states.

In deterministic latent dynamics, the transition is commonly expressed as (z_{t+1}=F_\\theta(z_t,a_t)). Given the same latent state and action, the model always produces the same prediction. Stochastic dynamics instead describe a conditional distribution such as (p_\\theta(z_{t+1}\|z_t,a_t)). The next state is therefore treated as a random variable whose possible values and probabilities depend on the current state and action.

This distinction is important because observations rarely reveal the complete physical state. A camera cannot see behind an obstacle, LiDAR measurements may be sparse or occluded, friction may be unknown, and another agent\'s intention may remain hidden. Two situations that appear almost identical to the robot can consequently evolve differently. A stochastic latent model can preserve these unresolved possibilities instead of prematurely collapsing them into one deterministic prediction.

Uncertainty also arises from the environment itself. A pedestrian approaching an intersection may continue forward, stop, or turn. An object being manipulated may slip or remain stable, and a mobile robot may experience slightly different motion because of terrain or contact conditions. Even with similar observations and actions, several futures can therefore be physically plausible. Stochastic dynamics provide a mathematical mechanism for representing this multimodality.

A common approach introduces a stochastic latent variable (z_t) whose distribution is parameterized by the model. Rather than producing a single vector, the network may predict parameters such as a mean (\\mu_t) and variance (\\sigma_t\^2), from which latent states can be sampled. More expressive models can use discrete variables, mixture distributions, hierarchical latents, or other probability structures when a simple unimodal Gaussian distribution cannot adequately represent alternative futures.

In recurrent state-space models, stochastic variables are often combined with deterministic recurrent memory. A hidden state (h_t) summarizes persistent temporal information, while (z_t) represents uncertain or variable aspects of the current situation. The transition may first update recurrent memory using previous state and action, then generate a prior distribution over the new stochastic latent variable. This combination provides both temporal continuity and probabilistic uncertainty.

Training typically distinguishes a predictive prior from an observation-conditioned posterior. The prior (p(z_t\|h_t)) predicts what latent state should occur using previous information, while the posterior (q(z_t\|h_t,o_t)) can additionally observe the actual sensory evidence. Because the posterior sees the observation, it can infer a more informed state. Learning encourages the prior to approximate this posterior so that useful futures can later be imagined without future observations.

This relationship is often optimized through a divergence term such as Kullback-Leibler divergence. The objective encourages the predictive prior and observation-conditioned posterior to remain compatible while reconstruction or prediction objectives require the sampled latent state to preserve useful information. The balance is important: excessive regularization can make latent variables uninformative, whereas insufficient regularization can produce a representation that is difficult to predict during imagination.

Sampling enables the world model to generate multiple possible futures from the same starting condition. Given (z_t) and an action sequence, the model can perform several stochastic rollouts, each producing a different plausible trajectory. One rollout might predict that another agent continues its current motion, while another predicts that it changes direction. Planning can evaluate this collection of futures rather than relying on a single imagined trajectory.

The resulting distribution of trajectories is particularly valuable for risk-aware decision making. An action may appear safe under the most likely future while becoming dangerous under a less probable but physically plausible outcome. By considering multiple stochastic futures, a planner can estimate collision probability, expected reward, uncertainty, or worst-case consequences. Physical AI can therefore select actions based not only on expected performance but also on the range of possible outcomes.

Stochastic latent dynamics are also useful for separating predictable structure from unpredictable variation. Geometry, robot kinematics, and persistent object motion may follow relatively stable patterns, while human intentions, contact outcomes, or hidden environmental factors may remain uncertain. A well-designed latent model can encode stable temporal information in deterministic components and reserve stochastic variables for aspects of the world that cannot be confidently predicted.

Long-horizon prediction amplifies the importance of probabilistic modeling. Uncertainty generally increases as predictions extend further into the future because each uncertain transition introduces additional possibilities. Instead of pretending that a distant future is precisely known, stochastic dynamics can propagate uncertainty through successive latent transitions. The predicted future may therefore expand from a narrow distribution at short horizons into a broader set of possibilities at longer horizons.

However, stochasticity should not become arbitrary randomness. A useful stochastic world model must produce diverse futures that remain consistent with observations, actions, temporal context, and physical constraints. Random variation that does not correspond to plausible environmental behavior provides little value for planning. Training must therefore encourage both diversity and fidelity so that sampled trajectories represent meaningful alternative futures rather than uncontrolled noise.

The representation of uncertainty can also guide active perception. If several latent hypotheses remain plausible because an important region is occluded or a physical property is unknown, the agent may choose an action that gathers additional information. Moving a camera, changing viewpoint, approaching an object, or slowing the robot can reduce uncertainty before a critical decision. The world model can therefore influence not only what action achieves a task but also what action improves knowledge.

For multimodal Physical AI, stochastic latent states can help manage disagreement or degradation among sensors. Cameras may become unreliable in darkness, LiDAR may suffer from environmental effects, radar may provide ambiguous geometry, and proprioceptive measurements may contain noise. Instead of treating fused information as perfectly certain, the latent model can maintain uncertainty about the underlying state and update that uncertainty as additional observations become available.

Evaluation of stochastic dynamics requires more than measuring error against one future trajectory. A model should assign high probability to futures that actually occur while maintaining appropriate diversity when several outcomes are possible. Calibration, likelihood-based measures, coverage of plausible futures, multi-step consistency, and downstream planning performance become important. A model that generates many possibilities is useful only when its probabilities and uncertainty meaningfully correspond to reality.

Within Physical AI, stochastic latent dynamics transform the world model from a single-future predictor into a system capable of reasoning about alternative possibilities. Combined with recurrent memory, action conditioning, uncertainty propagation, and multi-step rollout, they allow an agent to imagine not merely what will happen, but what could happen and how likely different outcomes may be. This provides a foundation for robust prediction, uncertainty-aware planning, safer control, and intelligent interaction in complex physical environments.

## 03.06. Discrete vs Continuous Latent State

![](images/image6.png){width="7.268055555555556in" height="7.268055555555556in"}

Latent dynamics models require a choice about how internal states should be represented mathematically. Two major alternatives are continuous and discrete latent states. A continuous representation places the state within a real-valued vector or feature space, whereas a discrete representation expresses it using symbols, categories, indices, or learned codebook entries. Both approaches compress observations, but they organize information and dynamics in fundamentally different ways.

A continuous latent state typically takes the form (z_t \\in \\mathbb{R}\^d), where each dimension contains a real-valued feature learned from observations. Nearby points in this space can represent similar physical situations, allowing gradual changes in position, velocity, orientation, geometry, force, or other properties to correspond to smooth movements through latent space. This makes continuous representations naturally compatible with many physical processes.

For example, a mobile robot changing velocity from one value to another does not usually pass through a sequence of sharply separated semantic categories. Its physical state changes continuously. An encoder can map these variations into nearby latent vectors, while a dynamics model learns a transition such as (z_{t+1}=F_\\theta(z_t,a_t)). Gradient-based learning can then optimize the encoder, dynamics model, and downstream components through differentiable latent representations.

Continuous states are especially useful when precise geometric or dynamical information must be preserved. Robot pose, joint angles, velocities, depth, contact forces, object trajectories, and spatial relationships naturally contain continuous quantities. A continuous latent space can encode combinations of these variables without requiring explicit discretization boundaries, supporting fine-grained prediction and control where small differences in state may produce meaningful differences in action outcomes.

However, continuous representations can encode information in highly distributed and difficult-to-interpret ways. A small movement in latent space does not necessarily correspond to a clearly identifiable semantic change, and models may allocate dimensions inefficiently or preserve irrelevant variations. Continuous states can also make long-horizon prediction difficult because small numerical errors introduced at each transition may accumulate as repeated latent rollouts move progressively away from states encountered during training.

Discrete latent states represent information using a finite or countable set of alternatives. Instead of directly storing an arbitrary real-valued vector, an encoder may select one or more entries from a learned codebook, producing indices or tokens that represent recurring patterns. The latent state can therefore be written conceptually as (z_t \\in {1,2,\\ldots,K}), although practical systems often use multiple discrete variables or sequences of tokens to achieve greater representational capacity.

Vector quantization is a common mechanism for constructing discrete latent representations. An encoder first produces a continuous feature, which is then mapped to the nearest or most appropriate codebook vector. The selected code represents the observation in a finite latent vocabulary. During learning, both the encoder and codebook can adapt so that frequently occurring structures, configurations, motions, or semantic patterns receive useful reusable representations.

Discrete states can encourage the model to organize experience into recurring concepts rather than arbitrary numerical variations. Different visual appearances of the same underlying configuration may map to the same or related latent codes. This can create a useful information bottleneck that suppresses insignificant sensory variation. Discrete tokens can also integrate naturally with autoregressive models and Transformer architectures that have demonstrated strong capabilities for modeling sequences of symbolic units.

For world modeling, tokenized states create the possibility of treating physical experience similarly to a structured sequence modeling problem. Spatial regions, objects, motions, actions, or compressed observation patches can be represented by tokens, and a predictive model can learn distributions over future token sequences. This approach can exploit scalable sequence-modeling architectures while avoiding the cost of predicting every pixel or sensor measurement directly.

Discretization nevertheless introduces important limitations. Physical variables are often inherently continuous, and mapping them into finite categories creates quantization error. Two states located near a code boundary may receive different tokens despite being physically similar, while distinct states may share one token if the codebook lacks sufficient resolution. Increasing the number of codes reduces some information loss but increases model complexity and can make the discrete representation harder to learn efficiently.

The distinction also affects uncertainty modeling. Continuous stochastic latent states can represent distributions using parameters such as means and variances, providing smooth probability densities over possible physical states. Discrete stochastic models instead predict probabilities over codes or token combinations. The latter can represent sharply separated alternatives naturally, such as whether an object moves left or right, while continuous distributions may better describe uncertainty in quantities such as position or velocity.

Long-horizon dynamics reveal different strengths of the two approaches. Continuous rollouts can preserve precise local changes but may gradually accumulate numerical drift. Discrete transitions constrain predictions to a learned set of valid codes, which can reduce some forms of uncontrolled latent drift. However, token prediction errors can produce abrupt transitions into incorrect states, and repeated discrete prediction can still accumulate semantic errors over long horizons.

Planning and control also impose different requirements. Low-level control often depends on precise continuous quantities, making continuous latent states attractive for motor prediction, trajectory optimization, and differentiable planning. Higher-level reasoning may benefit from discrete abstractions representing situations, behaviors, objects, skills, or interaction modes. A robot may therefore reason symbolically about what type of situation it is in while retaining continuous variables for exactly where objects are and how they are moving.

This motivates hybrid latent representations that combine continuous and discrete information rather than treating them as mutually exclusive choices. A world model might use discrete tokens to encode semantic structure, object identity, interaction modes, or high-level events while using continuous variables for geometry, motion, uncertainty, and robot configuration. The dynamics model can then predict changes at multiple representational levels corresponding to different aspects of the physical world.

Hierarchical world models can extend this idea further. Slowly changing discrete states may represent tasks, behaviors, scenes, or modes, while faster continuous states describe detailed physical evolution inside each mode. For example, a manipulation model could represent grasping, lifting, and placing as discrete phases while continuously modeling joint motion, contact forces, and object pose within each phase. Such structure can improve abstraction without sacrificing physical precision.

The appropriate representation therefore depends strongly on the prediction objective. If the model must reconstruct detailed sensor signals or predict precise physical trajectories, continuous latent variables may be preferable. If the goal emphasizes scalable sequence prediction, reusable abstractions, or semantic reasoning, discrete states may offer advantages. When both requirements are important, hybrid representations can distribute information according to the structure most appropriate for each variable.

Representation quality should consequently be evaluated through downstream behavior rather than by assuming that one latent type is universally superior. Prediction accuracy, quantization error, temporal consistency, uncertainty calibration, long-horizon stability, planning utility, computational efficiency, and control performance can reveal whether the chosen representation preserves the information that matters. The best latent state is ultimately the one that enables reliable reasoning and action.

Within Physical AI, continuous and discrete latent states represent complementary approaches to constructing an internal world. Continuous spaces capture smooth physical variation and fine-grained dynamics, while discrete spaces provide compressed reusable abstractions and tokenized structure. Their combination offers a path toward world models that can simultaneously reason about precise physical evolution and higher-level concepts, connecting perception, prediction, planning, and action across multiple levels of abstraction.

## 03.07. Representation Collapse and Regularization

![](images/image7.png){width="7.268055555555556in" height="7.268055555555556in"}

Representation collapse is a failure mode in latent dynamics models where the learned representation loses the diversity and information needed to distinguish meaningful states of the world. Instead of mapping different observations to appropriately different latent states, the encoder may produce nearly identical representations for many inputs. The latent space then becomes insufficient for prediction, planning, control, or understanding physical change.

A useful latent representation should compress irrelevant sensory variation while preserving distinctions that affect future dynamics. Collapse occurs when this compression becomes excessive. If observations containing different objects, positions, velocities, contacts, or environmental conditions are mapped to the same or very similar (z_t), the dynamics model cannot reliably determine which future transition should follow from the current internal state.

Complete collapse represents the extreme case in which an encoder produces almost the same latent vector for every observation. More subtle forms can also occur. Only a small subset of latent dimensions may carry information while the remaining dimensions become inactive, or discrete representations may repeatedly select only a few entries from a large codebook. Such partial collapse reduces the effective capacity of the representation even when its nominal dimensionality remains large.

Collapse can arise because some learning objectives admit trivial solutions. If training requires two related observations to have similar embeddings without simultaneously requiring representations to retain sufficient information, the easiest solution may be to map everything to the same point. Prediction objectives can exhibit related behavior when the target representation itself becomes uninformative, allowing the model to minimize error without learning meaningful physical structure.

Stochastic latent models face a related problem known as posterior collapse. When a powerful recurrent or autoregressive component can predict observations without using the stochastic latent variable, the posterior may become nearly identical to the prior and (z_t) may carry little useful information. The model technically contains a stochastic state, but the decoder or dynamics network effectively ignores it, weakening its ability to represent uncertainty and hidden factors.

Discrete latent models can experience codebook collapse. A codebook may contain hundreds or thousands of possible latent entries, yet the encoder may repeatedly choose only a small number of them. Unused codes receive little useful learning signal, while frequently selected codes become increasingly dominant. The resulting representation wastes available capacity and may fail to distinguish diverse physical situations that should occupy different regions of the latent vocabulary.

The opposite problem is also undesirable. Preventing collapse does not mean forcing the representation to preserve every detail of the observation. A latent state that memorizes textures, noise, illumination, and other unpredictable sensory variations may become unnecessarily difficult to model over time. The objective is therefore not maximum information retention but an appropriate balance between compression and preservation of dynamically meaningful information.

Regularization provides mechanisms for maintaining this balance. Rather than allowing the encoder and dynamics model to choose arbitrary latent organizations, regularization introduces constraints or auxiliary objectives that encourage informative, stable, and predictable representations. Different techniques regulate different properties, including variance, covariance, entropy, distribution shape, code usage, temporal consistency, and agreement between predicted and observed latent states.

Variance-based regularization can prevent embeddings from becoming constant by requiring latent dimensions to maintain sufficient variation across a batch of observations. If the variance of a feature approaches zero, the representation is carrying almost no discriminative information along that dimension. Encouraging nonzero variance helps ensure that different observations occupy distinguishable locations while still allowing the encoder to remove irrelevant variability.

Covariance regularization can complement this objective by discouraging different latent dimensions from carrying redundant information. Without such constraints, many dimensions may learn nearly identical features, producing an apparently large representation with low effective dimensionality. Reducing unnecessary correlations encourages the latent space to distribute information more efficiently and allows different dimensions or feature groups to capture complementary aspects of the physical state.

Contrastive learning offers another strategy by explicitly separating representations of different examples while bringing related observations closer together. Positive pairs may correspond to temporally related views, augmentations of the same scene, or observations representing the same underlying state. Negative relationships encourage distinguishability. However, the construction of positive and negative pairs must respect physical meaning so that useful state differences are not accidentally removed.

Non-contrastive and self-distillation approaches can prevent trivial collapse without requiring explicit negative examples. These methods may use asymmetric networks, stop-gradient operations, predictor modules, moving-average target encoders, or architectural constraints. One network predicts a representation generated by another slowly changing branch, while optimization is structured so that both branches cannot simply converge toward an uninformative constant representation.

Distributional regularization is particularly important for stochastic latent dynamics. Objectives such as Kullback-Leibler divergence can encourage posterior latent distributions to remain compatible with predictive priors. However, excessive pressure toward the prior can cause posterior collapse. Techniques such as KL balancing, free bits, minimum information constraints, or carefully weighted regularization can preserve useful stochastic information while maintaining a latent space that remains predictable during imagination.

Discrete representations require mechanisms that encourage healthy codebook utilization. Commitment losses can encourage encoder outputs to remain close to selected codes, while codebook updates adapt entries toward observed features. Entropy or usage regularization can discourage the model from relying on only a few codes. Reinitializing persistently unused entries or using improved assignment strategies can further prevent large portions of the discrete vocabulary from becoming inactive.

Temporal regularization is especially relevant to world models because latent states must represent an evolving physical process. Consecutive states should usually change in ways consistent with actual environmental dynamics rather than fluctuate arbitrarily. Temporal consistency objectives can encourage smooth or predictable evolution while still permitting abrupt changes when genuine physical events occur. The goal is structured temporal variation, not simply forcing adjacent states to be identical.

Multi-step prediction provides another powerful constraint. A representation that supports only reconstruction of the current observation may encode details that are useless for dynamics. Requiring latent states to remain predictive across several future steps encourages the encoder to preserve persistent variables such as motion, geometry, object relationships, and agent configuration. Representations are therefore shaped by what remains useful for explaining future evolution rather than only present appearance.

Action-conditioned prediction further constrains the latent state by requiring it to preserve information needed to distinguish the consequences of different actions. If two physical situations respond differently to steering, force, joint motion, or contact, collapsing them into the same representation creates prediction errors. Training on action-dependent transitions therefore encourages the model to retain controllable and physically consequential aspects of the environment.

Regularization strength must be carefully balanced because every constraint introduces a trade-off. Too little regularization can produce unstable, redundant, or collapsed representations, while excessive regularization can suppress useful information and reduce model flexibility. The appropriate balance depends on latent dimensionality, stochasticity, observation complexity, prediction horizon, model capacity, and whether the representation is intended primarily for reconstruction, prediction, planning, or control.

Representation quality should therefore be monitored directly rather than inferred only from training loss. Useful diagnostics include latent variance, effective dimensionality, codebook utilization, posterior-prior divergence, temporal predictability, reconstruction performance, multi-step prediction accuracy, and downstream planning or control results. A low training objective does not guarantee that the latent space has learned a rich and physically useful organization.

For Physical AI, preventing representation collapse is essential because the latent state serves as the interface between perception, memory, dynamics, prediction, and action. Regularization should preserve meaningful distinctions without forcing the model to reproduce every sensory detail. A successful latent space remains compact yet diverse, stable yet responsive, predictable yet informative, providing the internal structure required for robust world modeling and long-horizon interaction with physical environments.

## 03.08. Long Horizon Latent Rollouts

![](images/image8.png){width="7.268055555555556in" height="7.268055555555556in"}

Long-horizon latent rollouts allow a world model to project its internal state many steps into the future without repeatedly reconstructing complete sensory observations. Starting from the current latent state (z_t), the dynamics model recursively predicts (z_{t+1}, z_{t+2}, \..., z_{t+H}). This creates an imagined trajectory through latent space that represents how the physical world may evolve over an extended prediction horizon.

The fundamental advantage of latent rollout is computational efficiency. Predicting future camera images, LiDAR scans, radar measurements, and other high-dimensional observations at every step can be prohibitively expensive. By evolving compact latent states instead, the model concentrates computation on information relevant to dynamics and decision making. Decoding can be performed selectively when interpretable predictions are required.

Action-conditioned rollouts extend this process from passive forecasting to interactive prediction. Given a candidate sequence (a_t,a_{t+1},\...,a_{t+H-1}), the model recursively applies a transition such as (z_{t+k+1}=F_\\theta(z_{t+k},a_{t+k})). Different candidate action sequences therefore generate different imagined futures, allowing the agent to compare the consequences of actions before executing them in the physical environment.

This capability provides the foundation for imagination-based planning. A planner can generate multiple candidate action sequences, roll each sequence forward through the latent dynamics model, and evaluate the resulting trajectories according to objectives such as progress, safety, energy consumption, collision risk, reward, or task completion. Physical interaction is required only after the system has selected a promising action or short action sequence.

Long horizons are valuable because many important consequences are not visible in one-step prediction. A steering command may be safe immediately but place a mobile robot in a poor configuration several seconds later. A manipulator motion may initially approach an object correctly but create a future collision or singularity. Extended latent rollouts allow the model to evaluate delayed consequences that short-horizon prediction may overlook.

However, recursive prediction introduces a fundamental problem: prediction errors accumulate. During the first transition, the predicted latent state may differ only slightly from the state that would have been inferred from a real observation. The next prediction begins from this imperfect state, introducing another error. After many recursive transitions, these small deviations can compound and move the imagined trajectory far from physically realistic states.

This phenomenon is commonly described as model drift or compounding error. During training, the dynamics model often receives latent states generated from real observations, but during long rollout it must operate on its own predictions. The distribution of predicted states can gradually differ from the training distribution. Once the rollout enters unfamiliar latent regions, prediction accuracy may deteriorate further, producing a self-reinforcing divergence from reality.

The quality of the latent representation strongly influences long-horizon stability. If (z_t) contains unpredictable textures, noise, or other rapidly varying sensory details, the dynamics model must predict information that becomes increasingly difficult over time. Representations emphasizing persistent properties such as geometry, motion, object relationships, robot configuration, and interaction state can make repeated prediction substantially more stable and useful.

Multi-step training directly addresses this issue by optimizing the model over several predicted transitions rather than only one-step accuracy. Instead of evaluating only (z_{t+1}), training can compare predictions across (z_{t+1},\...,z_{t+H}) with corresponding future representations. This exposes the model to its own recursive predictions and encourages dynamics that remain coherent after repeated application rather than performing well only for immediate transitions.

The prediction horizon used during training creates an important trade-off. Very short horizons provide strong and relatively easy supervision but may fail to teach long-term stability. Extremely long horizons can make optimization difficult because uncertainty and prediction error naturally increase with time. Practical systems may therefore combine multiple horizons, gradually extend rollout length, or weight near-term and distant prediction objectives differently.

Stochastic latent dynamics become increasingly important as the horizon grows. A distant future is rarely represented adequately by one deterministic trajectory because uncertainty expands through successive transitions. Instead of predicting a single (z_{t+H}), the model can sample multiple latent trajectories representing different plausible outcomes. The rollout then becomes a distribution over futures rather than one increasingly uncertain path.

Uncertainty propagation allows planning to distinguish between trajectories that remain predictable and trajectories whose outcomes become highly uncertain. An action sequence with excellent expected reward may be undesirable if some plausible rollouts produce catastrophic outcomes. Risk-aware planning can therefore consider expected performance together with variance, collision probability, confidence, or worst-case consequences across sampled latent futures.

Temporal abstraction can further extend the effective planning horizon. Predicting every physical transition at the same fine temporal resolution may be unnecessary for distant futures. A hierarchical model can use detailed short-term dynamics for immediate control while representing longer-term evolution with slower latent transitions, skills, events, or abstract states. This allows planning to reason farther ahead without simulating every low-level movement explicitly.

Such hierarchical rollouts naturally connect continuous and discrete latent representations. Continuous states can describe precise near-term geometry, velocity, force, and robot configuration, while discrete or abstract states represent longer-term modes such as approaching, grasping, transporting, avoiding, or completing a task phase. Prediction can therefore operate at several temporal and representational scales within one world model.

Closed-loop execution provides another important safeguard against long-horizon drift. A robot may imagine many steps into the future for planning but execute only the first action or a short segment of the selected trajectory. New observations are then encoded to update the latent state, and planning is performed again. This receding-horizon process repeatedly anchors imagination to reality while retaining the benefits of long-range foresight.

For Physical AI, this distinction between planning horizon and execution horizon is essential. The model may reason several seconds, minutes, or task stages ahead while committing physically only to actions that can be safely reconsidered after new evidence arrives. Long-horizon rollout therefore does not imply blindly following a long open-loop prediction; it provides foresight within a continuously corrected perception-prediction-action cycle.

Efficient rollout also determines how many alternative futures can be evaluated within real-time computational limits. Compact latent states allow thousands of hypothetical transitions to be substantially cheaper than equivalent high-resolution sensory simulations. This computational advantage can be used to explore more candidate actions, sample uncertainty, increase planning depth, or evaluate multiple objectives while maintaining the response time required by embodied systems.

Evaluation must measure more than one-step prediction accuracy. A model can perform extremely well on the next state while becoming unusable after repeated transitions. Useful metrics therefore include multi-step latent error, trajectory consistency, decoded prediction quality, uncertainty calibration, physical plausibility, planning performance, and the rate at which rollout quality deteriorates with horizon. Downstream control success remains an especially important measure.

Long-horizon latent rollouts transform a latent dynamics model from a next-state predictor into an internal simulator for extended reasoning. Their effectiveness depends on compact predictive representations, stable recurrent dynamics, action conditioning, multi-step learning, uncertainty modeling, temporal abstraction, and repeated correction from observations. Together, these mechanisms enable Physical AI to imagine distant consequences while maintaining sufficient efficiency and reliability for real-world planning and control.

## 03.09. Latent Dynamics for Control and RL

![](images/image9.png){width="7.268055555555556in" height="7.268055555555556in"}

Latent dynamics connect world modeling directly to control by providing a compact predictive state in which an agent can evaluate the consequences of its actions. Instead of making decisions from raw images, LiDAR scans, or other high-dimensional observations, the system encodes sensory information into a latent state (z_t). This state becomes the basis for predicting future transitions, rewards, risks, and action outcomes.

The central transition model is action-conditioned, commonly expressed as (p_\\theta(z_{t+1}\\mid z_t,a_t)) or (z_{t+1}=F_\\theta(z_t,a_t)). The current latent state describes the agent and environment, while (a_t) represents a candidate control action. Because different actions produce different future states, the learned dynamics model functions as an internal simulator that can answer what may happen if a particular action is executed.

Control requires more than predicting the next state. The system must determine which predicted future is desirable. A reward model (r_\\psi(z_t,a_t)), cost model, value function, or task-specific prediction head can estimate progress, safety, energy consumption, collision risk, task completion, and other objectives. The latent dynamics model therefore predicts how the world changes, while these additional functions evaluate whether those changes are useful.

This structure naturally supports model predictive control. Starting from (z_t), the controller generates candidate action sequences and rolls each sequence forward through the learned latent dynamics. The predicted trajectories are evaluated according to rewards, costs, constraints, or terminal objectives. The controller selects the most promising sequence, executes only the first action or short segment, observes the environment again, updates the latent state, and replans.

Planning in latent space can be substantially cheaper than planning through full sensory observations. A robot does not need to synthesize high-resolution camera images or point clouds for every candidate action at every future step. Compact latent trajectories can instead represent the information needed to evaluate behavior. This efficiency allows more candidate trajectories, longer horizons, or richer uncertainty estimates to be considered within real-time computational constraints.

Latent dynamics also provide the foundation for model-based reinforcement learning(model-based RL). Conventional model-free reinforcement learning learns a policy or value function primarily from direct interaction experience. Model-based approaches additionally learn how the environment changes. Once a sufficiently accurate latent world model exists, large amounts of policy learning can occur through imagined transitions rather than requiring every learning step to involve the physical environment.

Experience collected from the real robot or simulation can be represented as transitions such as ((o_t,a_t,r_t,o_{t+1})). Observations are encoded into latent states, and the world model learns state transitions together with reward, termination, or other task-relevant predictions. The resulting model creates a compact internal environment in which the agent can repeatedly simulate action sequences and improve its behavior using previously collected experience.

A policy (\\pi_\\eta(a_t\\mid z_t)) maps the current latent state to an action distribution. During imagined learning, the policy selects actions inside the learned world model, the dynamics model predicts subsequent latent states, and reward predictions estimate the consequences. Repeating this process generates imagined trajectories from which the policy can learn without continuously requesting new physical interactions.

A value function (V_\\xi(z_t)) can estimate the expected future return from a latent state. During imagined rollouts, predicted rewards provide short-term information while the value function estimates consequences beyond the explicit rollout horizon. This combination allows reinforcement learning to reason over longer effective horizons without requiring the world model to simulate an extremely long trajectory for every policy update.

Actor-critic methods fit naturally into this architecture. The actor chooses actions from latent states, while the critic evaluates expected return. Both can be trained using trajectories generated by the latent dynamics model. The world model, actor, and critic therefore form complementary components: the model predicts what could happen, the critic estimates how valuable those futures are, and the actor learns which actions are likely to produce desirable outcomes.

Learning primarily from imagination can greatly improve sample efficiency. Physical robot interaction is expensive, slow, and potentially dangerous compared with neural-network computation. A transition collected once from the real environment can contribute to world-model learning and indirectly support many imagined policy updates. This makes latent model-based RL attractive for Physical AI systems where mechanical wear, energy consumption, human supervision, or safety restrict unrestricted exploration.

However, imagined learning is useful only when the learned dynamics remain sufficiently faithful to reality. A policy may discover actions that exploit inaccuracies in the world model rather than genuinely effective behavior. This phenomenon can produce excellent predicted rewards inside imagination but poor real-world performance. Model uncertainty, conservative objectives, regularization, real-world validation, and continuous data collection are therefore important safeguards.

Uncertainty-aware latent dynamics can explicitly represent multiple plausible outcomes. Instead of assuming that one action produces one deterministic future, stochastic transitions can generate distributions over (z_{t+1}). Policies and planners can evaluate expected reward together with variance, collision probability, uncertainty, or unfavorable outcomes. This is especially important for robots interacting with humans, uncertain terrain, movable objects, or partially observed environments.

Exploration can also be guided by the world model. An agent may select actions not only because they immediately increase task reward but also because they reduce uncertainty or reveal unfamiliar dynamics. Visiting uncertain states can provide information about friction, contact, object behavior, traversability, or other hidden physical properties. Reinforcement learning can therefore balance task achievement with information gathering and model improvement.

Latent representations used for control must preserve action-relevant information. A representation may reconstruct observations accurately while discarding subtle properties that determine how the environment responds to actions. Contact conditions, velocity, payload, friction, actuator state, geometry, and hidden interaction variables can be crucial. Action-conditioned prediction helps force the latent state to retain distinctions that produce different physical consequences.

Long-horizon latent rollouts allow reinforcement learning to evaluate delayed effects. An action may provide immediate reward while leading to a poor state later, whereas another may temporarily sacrifice progress to create a safer or more advantageous future configuration. Multi-step imagination allows policies to optimize cumulative return rather than reacting only to immediate outcomes, connecting predictive world modeling with goal-directed behavior.

Hierarchical control can extend this principle across multiple time scales. High-level latent states may represent goals, task phases, skills, or interaction modes, while lower-level states describe precise motion, geometry, contact, and actuator dynamics. Reinforcement learning can select high-level behaviors while lower-level controllers execute them, allowing the world model to connect long-term decision making with fast physical control.

Closed-loop interaction remains essential even when much learning occurs in imagination. The robot observes the real environment, updates its latent state, selects an action, executes it, and receives new observations and rewards. Differences between predicted and observed outcomes provide new learning signals. The world model, policy, and value function can consequently improve together as experience reveals previously unknown dynamics or model errors.

This produces a continuous learning cycle: sense, encode, predict, imagine, evaluate, act, observe, and learn. Real-world experience improves the latent dynamics model; the improved model generates better imagined trajectories; those trajectories improve the policy and value estimates; and the improved policy generates new physical experience. Latent dynamics therefore become not merely a prediction module but the computational center of an adaptive control and learning loop.

For Physical AI, the significance of latent dynamics for control and reinforcement learning lies in transforming prediction into purposeful action. The agent can use a compact internal world to ask what different actions might cause, evaluate their likely consequences, learn policies through imagined experience, and continuously correct those policies through reality. This integration enables more sample-efficient, adaptive, uncertainty-aware, and long-horizon autonomous behavior.

## 03.10. Latent Dynamics Model [w/Code]

![](images/image10.png){width="7.268055555555556in" height="7.268055555555556in"}

![](images/image11.png){width="7.268055555555556in" height="7.045833333333333in"}

A latent dynamics model provides a compact predictive representation of how an environment evolves through time. Instead of forecasting every future pixel, LiDAR return, or sensor value directly, the model encodes observations into latent states and predicts transitions within that compressed space. The resulting internal dynamics preserve information that is useful for understanding motion, interaction, uncertainty, and future consequences.

At time (t), an observation (o_t) is transformed by an encoder into a latent state (z_t). This state should summarize both the observable environment and relevant properties of the embodied agent. Depending on the application, it may implicitly represent geometry, object relationships, velocity, robot configuration, contact conditions, semantic context, and other variables required to predict how the physical situation will develop.

The central component is the transition or dynamics function. In its simplest deterministic form, it can be written as (z_{t+1}=F_\\theta(z_t,a_t)), where (a_t) represents the action performed by the agent. The model therefore learns not merely how the world changes naturally, but how its evolution depends on intervention. Different candidate actions applied to the same latent state can generate different predicted futures.

Latent dynamics can also be stochastic when the next state cannot be determined uniquely. A transition distribution such as (p_\\theta(z_{t+1}\\mid z_t,a_t)) represents multiple plausible outcomes rather than forcing one prediction. This is important when observations are incomplete, other agents behave unpredictably, contact outcomes vary, or hidden physical properties influence the future in ways that cannot be inferred with certainty.

Temporal memory becomes necessary when the current observation alone does not contain enough information to estimate the physical state. Recurrent state-space architectures can combine a deterministic hidden state (h_t) with a stochastic or deterministic latent state (z_t). Previous states preserve historical context, while new observations correct the model\'s internal belief, allowing information about motion or temporarily occluded objects to persist through time.

This leads to an important distinction between observation and imagination. During observation, the model combines predictions with actual sensory evidence to infer the current latent state. During imagination, future observations are unavailable, so the dynamics model recursively predicts future states using only its internal state and candidate actions. The same learned representation can therefore support both continuous state estimation and hypothetical future simulation.

A decoder may reconstruct observations from latent states, but full reconstruction is not always necessary. For control-oriented Physical AI, prediction heads can instead estimate task-relevant quantities such as occupancy, object motion, traversability, collision probability, reward, termination, or value. The latent representation should preserve what is necessary for prediction and action rather than reproducing every sensory detail that happened to appear in the original observation.

The mathematical structure of the latent state can be continuous, discrete, or hybrid. Continuous representations naturally encode smooth quantities such as position, velocity, force, and geometry. Discrete states can organize recurring patterns into learned codes or tokens that support abstraction and sequence modeling. Hybrid architectures can combine precise continuous dynamics with discrete representations of objects, events, behaviors, task phases, or interaction modes.

Representation learning is therefore inseparable from dynamics learning. If the encoder preserves excessive noise, texture, or unpredictable detail, future prediction becomes unnecessarily difficult. If compression is too aggressive, physically important distinctions disappear. A useful latent state selectively retains information that determines future evolution while suppressing variation that has little relevance to prediction, planning, or control.

Representation collapse is one major failure mode. The encoder may map different physical situations to nearly identical latent states, stochastic variables may become unused, or a discrete model may rely on only a small portion of its codebook. Variance constraints, covariance regularization, temporal consistency, distributional objectives, codebook utilization mechanisms, and carefully designed self-supervised losses can help preserve informative latent structure.

Training the dynamics model only for one-step prediction is often insufficient. A model may accurately predict (z_{t+1}) while becoming unstable when its own outputs are recursively used as inputs. Multi-step training exposes the system to sequences of predicted states and encourages consistency across longer horizons. This is essential because small transition errors can otherwise accumulate into substantial model drift during extended imagination.

Long-horizon latent rollout recursively applies the learned transition to generate (z_{t+1},z_{t+2},\...,z_{t+H}). Because these states are compact, many hypothetical trajectories can be simulated more efficiently than full high-dimensional sensory futures. Candidate action sequences can therefore be evaluated according to expected progress, reward, energy consumption, collision risk, uncertainty, task completion, or other objectives before physical execution.

Uncertainty generally increases as the rollout horizon becomes longer. Stochastic dynamics can propagate this uncertainty by generating multiple plausible trajectories rather than a single increasingly unreliable prediction. Planning can then consider not only the expected outcome but also variance, confidence, collision probability, or unfavorable alternatives. This transforms prediction from estimating what will happen into reasoning about what could happen under different actions.

Temporal abstraction can further improve long-range reasoning. Immediate physical control may require detailed latent transitions at high frequency, while distant planning can operate through slower-changing representations of events, skills, goals, or task phases. Hierarchical latent dynamics can therefore connect fast continuous physical evolution with slower abstract transitions, allowing the same world model to reason across multiple spatial, temporal, and semantic scales.

For control, latent dynamics function as an internal simulator. Model predictive control can generate candidate action sequences, roll them forward in latent space, evaluate their predicted consequences, execute a short portion of the best sequence, and then replan after receiving new observations. This receding-horizon strategy combines long-range foresight with continuous correction from reality, reducing the danger of blindly following inaccurate open-loop predictions.

The same architecture provides a foundation for model-based reinforcement learning. Real experience can train the latent world model, after which policies and value functions can learn from imagined trajectories generated internally. The model predicts possible consequences, a critic evaluates their expected value, and an actor learns actions that produce desirable outcomes. One physical experience can consequently support many computational learning updates.

However, a learned latent simulator must never be assumed to be perfectly faithful. Policies can exploit inaccuracies in the model, and prediction errors can become increasingly severe outside the training distribution. Reliable systems therefore require uncertainty estimation, conservative planning, continuous comparison between predicted and observed outcomes, additional data collection, and repeated updating of the world model as previously unknown situations are encountered.

The complete latent dynamics model ultimately forms a continuous cycle connecting perception, memory, prediction, imagination, evaluation, and action. Sensors update the current internal state; dynamics project that state into possible futures; planning or reinforcement learning evaluates alternatives; actions modify the physical world; and subsequent observations correct the internal model. Prediction and interaction thereby become parts of one closed-loop computational process.

For Physical AI, the significance of the latent dynamics model is that it converts high-dimensional sensory experience into a compact internal world that can evolve independently of immediate observation. Within this internal space, an embodied agent can remember hidden state, predict physical change, simulate alternative actions, represent uncertainty, plan across longer horizons, and learn from imagined experience before committing actions to the real environment.
