# Chapter 00. Introduction

## 00.01. Why World Models Matter for Physical AI

![](images/image1.png){width="7.268055555555556in" height="7.268055555555556in"}

Physical AI operates in environments that are continuous, dynamic, partially observable, and governed by physical constraints. Unlike digital AI, which can often reason over static information or symbolic representations, a physical agent must understand how the surrounding world changes over time. A world model provides the internal structure needed to represent these changing conditions and connect perception with intelligent action.

A robot never observes the physical world directly in its entirety. Cameras provide projected images, LiDAR measures sparse geometric surfaces, radar estimates range and velocity, and proprioceptive sensors describe the robot's own motion. These observations are incomplete and sometimes noisy. A world model integrates them into an internal representation that approximates the underlying state of the environment beyond any individual sensor measurement.

This internal representation becomes particularly important because physical intelligence requires temporal continuity. A robot must relate what it observes now to what it observed moments earlier and anticipate what may happen next. Moving vehicles, pedestrians, manipulators, doors, terrain, and even the robot itself evolve through time. World models therefore transform isolated observations into structured estimates of state, motion, interaction, and possible future evolution.

Prediction is one of the central reasons world models matter for Physical AI. Reactive systems respond primarily to current sensor inputs, while predictive systems can estimate future states before they occur. If a mobile robot predicts that a pedestrian will enter its path, or a manipulator predicts that an object will slip after contact, the system can modify its behavior before the undesirable event becomes unavoidable.

World models also allow an agent to reason about the consequences of its own actions. Physical actions alter the environment, making perception and control inseparable. Steering changes future camera views, acceleration changes collision risk, and grasping changes object configurations. An action-conditioned world model can estimate how different candidate actions may transform future states, enabling the robot to compare alternatives before executing them physically.

This ability introduces a form of internal simulation. Instead of testing every possibility in the real world, an agent can generate predicted trajectories or latent state transitions inside its model. Candidate actions can be imagined, evaluated, rejected, or refined without immediately affecting the environment. Such internal rollouts provide an important computational bridge from prediction toward planning, model predictive control, and model-based reinforcement learning.

World models are equally important for dealing with partial observability. Objects may disappear behind obstacles, sensors may temporarily fail, and important environmental properties may not be directly measurable. By maintaining memory and a belief about hidden states, the system can preserve information across time. The robot therefore acts not only on what is currently visible, but also on what its accumulated experience suggests is likely to exist.

For Physical AI, useful world representations must capture more than geometry. Spatial structure identifies where things are, semantic information indicates what they are, dynamics describe how they move, and physical properties influence how they interact. Traversability, contact, friction, support, velocity, object permanence, free space, occupancy, and uncertainty can all become relevant components of an internal state depending on the task and embodiment.

This requirement explains why world modeling can take many representational forms. Some systems use explicit maps, object states, occupancy fields, or bird's-eye-view representations, while others encode the environment into learned latent variables. The appropriate representation depends on the information needed for prediction and action. A world model does not necessarily need to reconstruct every observable detail; it must preserve information that supports effective physical reasoning.

Efficient abstraction is especially valuable because Physical AI systems operate under limited computational, power, memory, and latency budgets. Predicting every future pixel at full resolution may consume substantial resources while representing details irrelevant to control. Latent predictive approaches instead attempt to encode task-relevant structure and predict changes in representation space, potentially allowing physical agents to reason about future situations without reproducing complete sensory observations.

Physical environments also contain uncertainty that cannot be eliminated simply by increasing model capacity. Sensors are imperfect, future human behavior is ambiguous, terrain properties may be unknown, and multiple future outcomes can remain plausible. A useful world model therefore needs mechanisms for representing uncertainty rather than producing only a single confident prediction. This becomes critical when predictions are used for collision avoidance, manipulation, navigation, or other safety-related decisions.

World models can further improve learning efficiency because much of physical experience contains useful structure without explicit human labels. Temporal sequences naturally reveal how scenes evolve, how actions influence observations, and which properties remain stable. Self-supervised predictive learning can exploit large quantities of robot sensor data by learning representations from relationships across space, time, modalities, and actions rather than depending exclusively on manually labeled datasets.

Multimodal integration strengthens this capability. Physical robots commonly combine cameras, LiDAR, radar, IMUs, joint states, force sensing, positioning systems, and other measurements. Each modality captures different aspects of reality and fails in different ways. A multimodal world model can align these signals within a shared spatial-temporal representation, allowing complementary observations to support more robust estimates of environmental and robot state.

The importance of world models becomes even greater as robots move from structured facilities into open environments. Warehouses can provide relatively stable geometry and predictable traffic rules, whereas roads, construction sites, farms, disaster zones, and outdoor terrain contain changing surfaces, uncertain objects, weather effects, and novel situations. Fixed rules become increasingly insufficient as environmental diversity and interaction complexity increase.

Generalization therefore depends partly on whether an agent has learned reusable structure about how physical environments behave. Memorizing correlations between observations and actions may work inside familiar distributions but fail when conditions change. World models seek to capture more transferable regularities involving motion, persistence, causality, geometry, interaction, and physical constraints, providing representations that can potentially support behavior beyond specific training scenarios.

This also creates an important connection between world models and simulation. Traditional simulators begin with manually specified physical rules and generate trajectories from those rules, while learned world models infer useful transition structure from data. Hybrid approaches can combine analytical physics with learned residual dynamics, enabling known constraints to provide structure while data-driven components model effects that are difficult to specify accurately.

For planning, the value of a world model ultimately depends on whether its predictions improve decisions. A visually impressive future prediction is insufficient if it omits information necessary for safe control. Physical AI therefore requires world models whose representations and dynamics are aligned with downstream tasks. Prediction quality, uncertainty, computational cost, temporal horizon, and planning utility must be considered together rather than evaluated as independent objectives.

World models also provide a foundation for increasingly general robot intelligence. If representations can be learned across tasks, environments, sensor configurations, and embodiments, they may evolve from task-specific predictive systems toward broader physical foundation models. Shared models could capture reusable concepts such as objects, free space, motion, contact, support, affordances, and action consequences while specialized task heads adapt those representations to individual robotic platforms.

In this sense, world models occupy a central position in the architecture of Physical AI. Perception estimates the present, memory connects the past, predictive dynamics construct possible futures, planning evaluates those futures, and control turns selected possibilities into physical actions. The world model provides the internal computational space in which these functions can interact, making it a key mechanism for moving from reactive automation toward predictive, adaptive, and increasingly general physical intelligence.

## 00.02. From Perception to Internal World Representation

![](images/image2.png){width="7.268055555555556in" height="7.268055555555556in"}

Physical AI begins its interaction with the world through perception, but perception alone does not constitute understanding. Sensors continuously produce images, point clouds, ranges, inertial measurements, forces, joint states, and other signals. These measurements describe fragments of the environment at particular moments. To support intelligent behavior, they must be transformed into a persistent internal representation of the world.

The distinction between observation and world state is fundamental. An observation is what the sensors currently measure, whereas the world state represents what the agent believes exists in the environment. A camera may observe only one side of an object, while the internal representation may preserve its estimated position, identity, size, motion, and previously observed properties even when the object becomes temporarily invisible.

This transformation begins with sensory encoding. Raw camera pixels, LiDAR points, radar returns, audio signals, inertial measurements, and proprioceptive states exist in different mathematical spaces and resolutions. Encoders convert these heterogeneous signals into structured features or latent representations. The objective is not merely compression, but extraction of information useful for understanding geometry, semantics, motion, physical interaction, and future behavior.

Physical AI typically requires multimodal perception because no individual sensor provides a complete description of reality. Cameras capture rich appearance and semantic information but provide indirect geometry. LiDAR measures accurate spatial structure but contains limited appearance information. Radar provides robust range and velocity measurements, while IMUs and proprioception describe the agent's own movement. Their combination creates a more complete estimate of the world.

Sensor fusion therefore represents an important transition from perception toward world representation. Measurements must be aligned spatially and temporally before they can describe a common physical scene. Calibration establishes relationships between sensor coordinate systems, while synchronization associates measurements occurring at approximately the same time. Without these relationships, observations from different sensors may describe incompatible versions of the same environment.

The robot must also distinguish changes caused by the environment from changes caused by its own motion. When a mobile robot turns, almost every camera pixel and LiDAR point can move even if surrounding objects remain stationary. Ego-motion estimation allows observations to be transformed into a consistent reference frame. This separation between self-motion and environmental motion is essential for constructing stable representations of dynamic physical scenes.

Once observations are aligned, the system can organize them into spatial representations. Depending on the application, these may include metric maps, bird's-eye-view representations, occupancy grids, three-dimensional voxel structures, object-centric states, neural fields, or latent spatial embeddings. Each representation preserves different aspects of reality, and the appropriate choice depends on what downstream prediction, planning, and control require.

Geometry alone, however, is insufficient for intelligent physical interaction. The system must increasingly associate spatial structure with semantic meaning. A geometric region may correspond to a wall, road, pedestrian, vehicle, pallet, doorway, table, tool, or manipulable object. Semantic representation converts anonymous physical structure into entities and regions whose meaning can influence navigation, interaction, task planning, and safety decisions.

Object-centric representations provide another useful level of abstraction. Instead of treating the world entirely as pixels or grid cells, an agent can represent persistent entities with attributes such as position, orientation, dimensions, velocity, category, and interaction state. Such representations can simplify reasoning because many physical tasks involve relationships among objects, agents, surfaces, and the robot rather than relationships among individual sensor measurements.

A world representation must also include dynamics. Physical environments are not static maps but evolving systems in which objects move, agents act, and interactions change future conditions. Estimating velocity, acceleration, trajectories, articulation, contact, and other dynamic properties allows the internal model to distinguish what currently exists from how that state is changing. This distinction prepares the representation for future-state prediction.

Temporal integration is therefore central to the transition from perception to a world model. A single observation may be ambiguous, but a sequence of observations provides information about persistence and motion. Temporal memory allows evidence to accumulate across frames, reduces sensitivity to temporary sensor noise, and preserves information about objects that become occluded. The internal representation consequently becomes more stable than any individual observation.

This process naturally leads to the concept of a belief state. Because the physical world is only partially observable, the internal state cannot always be treated as a perfect description of reality. Instead, it represents the agent's best estimate based on current measurements, previous observations, learned dynamics, and uncertainty. The belief state may contain both estimated properties and confidence about whether those properties are correct.

Uncertainty is therefore part of representation rather than merely an error added after perception. Sensor noise, occlusion, ambiguous classifications, unknown terrain, and unpredictable agents create different levels of confidence across the world state. Representing these uncertainties allows downstream planning systems to distinguish well-observed free space from poorly observed regions or highly predictable motion from behavior with multiple plausible futures.

Learned latent representations provide an alternative to explicitly specifying every component of the world state. Neural encoders can transform high-dimensional observations into compact latent variables that preserve information useful for prediction or control. Such representations can capture relationships that are difficult to design manually and can reduce computational cost when future states are predicted directly within the learned representation space.

Explicit and latent representations are not necessarily competing approaches. A Physical AI architecture may maintain interpretable geometric structures such as occupancy or BEV maps while simultaneously learning latent features that encode semantics, dynamics, or interaction patterns. Hybrid representations can combine the transparency and physical grounding of explicit states with the flexibility and representational capacity of learned latent spaces.

The quality of an internal representation should ultimately be judged by its usefulness rather than by how completely it reconstructs sensory input. Information relevant to future prediction, collision avoidance, navigation, manipulation, or interaction should be preserved, while irrelevant details may be discarded. This principle makes world representation task-aware without requiring the model to encode every property of the physical environment with equal precision.

Action also influences how the world should be represented. A robot does not passively observe its environment; it changes its observations and surroundings through movement and interaction. Internal states that support Physical AI should therefore preserve variables that help predict action consequences. Position, velocity, contact state, traversability, object affordances, and physical constraints become valuable because they determine how possible actions may transform the world.

As representations accumulate over longer periods, they can extend beyond immediate perception into world memory. Local observations can be associated with previously visited places, persistent objects, learned environmental structures, or recurring interaction patterns. Such memory enables the robot to reason beyond the current sensor horizon and creates continuity between short-term perception, spatial mapping, episodic experience, and long-term knowledge.

The transition from perception to internal world representation can therefore be understood as a progressive transformation from raw measurements into structured, temporally coherent, action-relevant beliefs about reality. Encoding extracts features, fusion combines modalities, spatial reasoning organizes geometry, semantics assigns meaning, memory preserves continuity, and uncertainty expresses what remains unknown. Together these processes create the internal state upon which prediction and planning can operate.

For Physical AI, this internal representation is the boundary between sensing the world and reasoning about it. Perception answers what appears to be happening now, while the world representation attempts to describe what exists, what has persisted, what is changing, and what remains uncertain. Once such a representation is available, the system can move beyond recognition toward predicting future states, evaluating possible actions, and selecting behavior within the physical world.

## 00.03. Prediction Planning and Action

![](images/image3.png){width="7.268055555555556in" height="7.268055555555556in"}

Physical AI becomes genuinely useful when an internal representation of the world can be converted into intelligent behavior. Building a representation explains what the agent believes about the present, but autonomous operation requires an additional capability: anticipating how the world may evolve and determining what should be done next. Prediction, planning, and action therefore form a continuous decision loop built on top of the world model.

Prediction extends the internal state from the present into possible futures. Given a current state, recent history, environmental context, and potentially an action, a world model estimates how relevant variables may change over time. These variables can include object positions, velocities, occupancy, semantic states, contact conditions, robot motion, or learned latent features. The prediction horizon may range from fractions of a second to many seconds or longer.

Short-horizon prediction is especially important for fast physical interaction. Collision avoidance, balance control, dynamic manipulation, and vehicle motion may require anticipating events only moments ahead. Longer-horizon prediction supports route selection, task sequencing, interaction planning, and strategic behavior. Physical AI therefore benefits from multi-horizon models that represent immediate dynamics while maintaining sufficient structure for decisions extending further into the future.

Prediction becomes more powerful when it is conditioned on action. The future does not evolve independently of the robot because the robot itself is part of the physical system. A mobile platform can accelerate, brake, or steer; a manipulator can reach, grasp, push, or release. An action-conditioned world model estimates how the environment and the robot state will change when a particular action or sequence of actions is executed.

This capability allows the agent to ask a fundamental planning question: "What will happen if I do this?" Different candidate actions can be propagated through the world model to generate alternative future trajectories. One steering command may lead through free space, another may approach an obstacle, and another may produce excessive risk. Planning can then compare these predicted consequences rather than selecting actions only from current observations.

Such predictive rollouts create an internal simulation process. The robot does not need to physically execute every candidate behavior to determine whether it is promising. Instead, possible futures can be generated inside the world model, evaluated according to objectives and constraints, and discarded when they appear unsafe or inefficient. Internal simulation can therefore reduce the cost and danger associated with physical trial and error.

Planning transforms predicted futures into decisions. A planner defines candidate trajectories, action sequences, or policies and evaluates them according to task objectives. Depending on the system, evaluation may consider distance to a goal, travel time, energy consumption, collision probability, manipulation success, stability, comfort, safety margins, or expected reward. The selected plan represents the future behavior that best satisfies the relevant objectives and constraints.

Model Predictive Control provides a direct example of this relationship. The system predicts the consequences of multiple control sequences over a finite horizon, selects a desirable sequence, executes only the immediate portion, and then repeats the process using new observations. This repeated replanning allows the agent to adapt when predictions are imperfect or when unexpected environmental changes occur.

Latent-space planning extends the same principle to learned representations. Instead of predicting every future sensor measurement, a model can roll forward compact latent states that encode task-relevant information. Candidate actions can then be evaluated within this representation space. If the latent state preserves geometry, dynamics, semantics, and action consequences sufficiently well, planning may become significantly more computationally efficient.

Physical planning must account for uncertainty because the future is rarely represented by a single guaranteed trajectory. A pedestrian may continue walking, stop, or change direction. An object being grasped may remain stable or slip. Terrain may provide uncertain traction. Probabilistic world models can represent several plausible futures or distributions over future states, allowing planning to consider both expected outcomes and associated risks.

Risk-aware planning becomes particularly important when prediction errors have physical consequences. A digital system may recover from an incorrect output with little cost, while a robot can collide with equipment, damage an object, injure a person, or destabilize itself. Physical AI should therefore prefer actions that remain acceptable across plausible futures rather than optimizing exclusively for the most likely predicted outcome.

Planning is also constrained by embodiment. A predicted path may be geometrically clear but physically impossible for a particular robot. Wheel steering limits, acceleration, braking distance, manipulator reachability, joint limits, payload, balance, contact forces, and actuator dynamics determine which futures are achievable. Effective world-model-based planning must therefore connect environmental prediction with the capabilities and constraints of the physical body.

Once a plan has been selected, action converts internal computation into physical change. High-level decisions must ultimately become trajectories, velocities, torques, steering angles, grasp commands, or other actuator-level signals. Control systems execute these commands while compensating for disturbances and modeling errors. Action is therefore not the endpoint of intelligence but the point where internal prediction encounters the actual dynamics of the world.

Every action generates new observations. After the robot moves, the cameras see a different scene, LiDAR produces new geometry, proprioceptive sensors report updated motion, and contact sensors may reveal unexpected interactions. These observations update the internal world representation and provide evidence about whether previous predictions were correct. Prediction, planning, and action consequently operate as a closed feedback loop rather than as a one-way pipeline.

Prediction errors are particularly valuable because they reveal mismatches between the internal model and reality. If the world evolves differently from what was expected, the system can update its state estimate, revise its dynamics model, increase uncertainty, or adapt future behavior. Repeated interaction therefore allows the world model to become better aligned with the environment while enabling the planner to compensate for remaining model imperfections.

This feedback structure supports adaptive autonomy. A robot can continuously observe the world, update its internal state, imagine possible futures, select an action, execute it, and observe the result. The cycle may operate at different frequencies across the architecture: low-level control can run rapidly, local planning at intermediate rates, and higher-level task reasoning over longer intervals. Together they form a hierarchy of predictive decision processes.

The same framework naturally connects world models with model-based reinforcement learning. Instead of learning only a direct mapping from observations to actions, an agent can learn environmental dynamics and use the learned model to evaluate behavior. Simulated experience generated inside the model can complement real-world experience, potentially reducing the number of costly physical interactions required to learn effective policies.

However, prediction accuracy alone does not guarantee successful planning. A model may reconstruct visually realistic futures while failing to preserve small but decision-critical properties such as collision boundaries, contact states, friction, or object motion. For Physical AI, world models should therefore be optimized and evaluated according to their ability to support decisions, not merely according to the visual or statistical fidelity of generated observations.

Computational efficiency is equally important because planning often requires evaluating many possible futures under strict latency constraints. Predictive models must balance representation richness, prediction horizon, uncertainty estimation, and inference cost. Hierarchical approaches can allocate detailed computation to nearby or safety-critical events while using more abstract predictions for distant possibilities, allowing limited compute to focus on information with the greatest decision value.

Prediction, planning, and action ultimately form the operational core of a world-model-based Physical AI system. Prediction answers what may happen, planning determines which future should be pursued, and action changes the physical world toward that future. New observations then correct the internal model and begin the cycle again. Through this continuous loop, a robot moves beyond reacting to current inputs and begins acting according to anticipated consequences.

The progression from perception to representation, prediction, planning, and action therefore describes a fundamental architecture for increasingly capable Physical AI. The world model serves as the internal environment in which possible futures can be constructed before they are physically realized. As these models become more accurate, uncertainty-aware, action-conditioned, and computationally efficient, they can support safer, more adaptive, and increasingly general autonomous behavior.

## 00.04. World Models vs Digital Twins

![](images/image4.png){width="7.268055555555556in" height="7.268055555555556in"}

![](images/image5.png){width="7.268055555555556in" height="7.268055555555556in"}

World models and digital twins both create computational representations of the physical world, but they are designed for different purposes. A digital twin typically represents a specific physical asset, process, facility, or system with sufficient fidelity for monitoring, simulation, engineering, and lifecycle management. A world model instead represents the environment as an intelligent agent needs to understand it for prediction, planning, and action.

A digital twin usually maintains an explicit relationship with a real-world counterpart. A factory line, robot, vehicle, building, turbine, or production system may have a corresponding digital representation containing geometry, configuration, operating parameters, sensor measurements, and historical information. The objective is often to maintain a synchronized virtual representation that supports analysis of the particular physical system throughout its operational lifecycle.

World models have a different center of gravity. Their primary purpose is not necessarily to reproduce a particular physical asset accurately, but to provide an agent with an internal representation sufficient for intelligent behavior. The model must capture what exists, how relevant entities are changing, what may happen next, and how possible actions could influence future states. Prediction and decision utility therefore become central design requirements.

This difference can be understood through perspective. A digital twin is commonly system-centric or asset-centric, while a world model is generally agent-centric. The digital twin asks how a particular physical system is configured and behaving. The world model asks what an autonomous agent needs to know about itself and its surroundings in order to anticipate consequences, achieve objectives, and interact safely with the physical environment.

Representation also differs significantly. Digital twins often rely on explicit engineering structures such as CAD geometry, kinematic models, physical parameters, equipment hierarchies, process models, maintenance records, and sensor databases. World models may use explicit structures as well, but they increasingly employ learned representations such as latent states, neural occupancy fields, BEV features, object embeddings, or multimodal representations learned directly from experience.

The source of knowledge provides another distinction. A digital twin can be constructed from engineering specifications, physical equations, system identification, operational databases, and sensor telemetry. Much of its structure may therefore be defined before operation begins. A learned world model can instead acquire substantial portions of its representation and dynamics from observational and interaction data through supervised, self-supervised, predictive, or reinforcement learning.

Synchronization with reality is important to both approaches, although its meaning differs. Digital twins frequently synchronize measured parameters with their physical counterparts to maintain an updated virtual instance of a particular system. World models continuously update internal belief states from observations because autonomous agents operate under partial observability. Their update process must combine perception, memory, prediction, and uncertainty rather than simply synchronize known variables.

Prediction exists in both technologies but serves different operational goals. A digital twin may predict component degradation, energy consumption, thermal behavior, production throughput, structural response, or maintenance requirements. A world model predicts information directly relevant to an agent's future interaction, such as object motion, occupancy changes, contact outcomes, traversability, future observations, or the consequences of candidate actions.

Action conditioning is therefore especially important for world models. Physical AI needs to estimate not only how the environment may evolve naturally, but how it may evolve differently depending on the robot's behavior. Steering left, braking, grasping an object, pushing a door, or changing velocity produces different futures. A world model can represent these counterfactual possibilities and allow a planner to evaluate them before physical execution.

Digital twins can also support what-if simulation, optimization, and control, so the distinction should not be interpreted as an absolute boundary. Advanced digital twins may incorporate dynamic simulation, machine learning, optimization, and closed-loop control. Similarly, world models may contain explicit physics and detailed geometric representations. The technologies increasingly overlap as both become more data-driven, predictive, and connected to real-time physical systems.

The difference is often clearer when considering fidelity. Digital twins may require high fidelity for the particular engineering quantities being analyzed. A structural twin may need accurate loads and material properties, while a manufacturing twin may preserve detailed process states. A world model can intentionally discard information that is irrelevant to the agent if the remaining representation preserves the variables required for prediction, planning, safety, and control.

This creates an important distinction between physical fidelity and decision-relevant fidelity. Reconstructing every detail of a warehouse may be unnecessary for an autonomous mobile robot if its task requires only free space, obstacles, dynamic agents, traversability, localization references, and task-relevant semantics. A world model can therefore be highly abstract while still being effective, provided that its abstractions support correct predictions and useful decisions.

Temporal scale can differ as well. Digital twins frequently accumulate information over long operational periods, supporting lifecycle analysis from commissioning through maintenance and retirement. World models may operate at much faster decision timescales, continuously predicting fractions of a second or several seconds ahead. However, world memory can also extend over long periods, allowing Physical AI systems to combine immediate dynamics with persistent environmental knowledge.

Uncertainty has a particularly important role in autonomous world modeling. An agent rarely has complete knowledge of its surroundings, and several futures may remain plausible. World models therefore benefit from representing uncertainty in observations, hidden states, dynamics, and future outcomes. Digital twins can also quantify uncertainty, but many traditional implementations begin from comparatively well-defined engineering models and instrumented system states.

Another difference concerns generalization. A digital twin is often closely associated with a specific asset or class of engineered systems. A world model may instead be trained to transfer knowledge across environments, tasks, or even different robot embodiments. A sufficiently general model could learn reusable concepts of motion, geometry, contact, objects, affordances, and physical interactions that apply across many platforms rather than describing only one physical counterpart.

The relationship between simulation and reality also differs conceptually. A digital twin often provides a virtual counterpart in which engineers can inspect, test, optimize, or predict the behavior of a real system. A world model functions more like an internal predictive environment for an autonomous agent. The agent can use it to imagine possible futures, evaluate candidate actions, and select behavior without explicitly constructing a complete engineering replica of its surroundings.

Despite these differences, digital twins can provide valuable infrastructure for developing world models. High-quality digital twins can generate simulated trajectories, physical interactions, failure scenarios, and synthetic sensor data. These experiences can support world-model pretraining and evaluation, particularly when collecting equivalent real-world data would be expensive, dangerous, or rare. The twin can therefore operate as an external learning environment for the internal model.

The relationship can also operate in the opposite direction. Learned world models can enhance digital twins by estimating unknown dynamics, correcting modeling errors, predicting complex interactions, or representing phenomena that are difficult to formulate analytically. Hybrid systems may combine explicit simulation with learned residual models, enabling engineering knowledge to define known structure while machine learning captures effects that conventional modeling does not represent adequately.

For Physical AI, an especially powerful architecture may therefore use both technologies rather than choosing between them. A digital twin can provide an external, persistent representation of a robot, facility, fleet, or operating environment, while each autonomous agent maintains an internal world model optimized for real-time prediction and decision making. Information can flow between these representations without requiring them to be identical.

In a fleet of robots, for example, a facility-level digital twin could maintain persistent maps, infrastructure status, operational constraints, robot locations, and historical information. Individual robots could maintain local world models containing immediate observations, dynamic objects, uncertainty, predicted trajectories, and candidate action consequences. Shared information could update the broader twin while the twin provides contextual knowledge to individual agents.

This distinction becomes increasingly important as Physical AI scales from individual robots to complex cyber-physical systems. Digital twins provide engineering continuity, system-level visibility, and persistent external representations, while world models provide adaptive internal intelligence for agents acting under uncertainty. Their combination can connect long-term system knowledge with short-term autonomous prediction and control.

World models and digital twins should therefore be viewed as complementary concepts with overlapping technologies but different primary objectives. The digital twin primarily asks how a physical counterpart can be represented, monitored, simulated, and managed. The world model primarily asks what an intelligent physical agent must represent and predict in order to choose effective actions. Understanding this distinction clarifies why both can occupy important but different roles in advanced Physical AI architectures.

## 00.05. World Models vs Foundation Models

![](images/image6.png){width="7.268055555555556in" height="7.268055555555556in"}

World models and foundation models represent two important but distinct directions in modern artificial intelligence. Foundation models emphasize broad knowledge and transferable capabilities learned from large and diverse datasets, while world models emphasize structured representations of environments and their evolution over time. For Physical AI, understanding how these approaches differ and complement each other is essential for building increasingly general autonomous systems.

A foundation model is typically trained at large scale so that its learned representations can support many downstream tasks. Instead of developing a separate model from scratch for every application, a common pretrained model can be adapted through prompting, fine-tuning, task-specific heads, or additional training. This paradigm has transformed language and vision and is increasingly being extended toward multimodal and robotic intelligence.

The defining property of a world model is different. A world model attempts to represent the state, dynamics, and possible evolution of an environment. It connects observations across time and may estimate how current conditions transition into future states. For an embodied agent, it can additionally model how actions change those transitions, allowing the system to reason about possible consequences before physically executing a behavior.

The distinction can therefore be expressed as general knowledge versus predictive environmental structure. Foundation models seek reusable representations across many tasks, domains, or modalities. World models seek representations that preserve information needed to understand and predict how a world changes. These goals overlap, but neither automatically guarantees the other. A broadly pretrained model is not necessarily a predictive model of physical dynamics.

Large language models illustrate this distinction clearly. They can encode extensive semantic, conceptual, and procedural knowledge acquired from text, yet textual competence alone does not provide a sufficiently grounded model of contact, friction, object permanence, robot dynamics, traversability, or sensor uncertainty. Physical AI requires knowledge that is connected not only to descriptions of reality but also to observations, actions, and physical consequences.

Robot foundation models extend the foundation-model paradigm toward embodied systems. They can be pretrained using data collected from multiple tasks, environments, robots, demonstrations, images, language instructions, and action trajectories. The objective is to acquire representations and policies that transfer across tasks. Vision-language-action models further connect visual observations and language instructions with robot actions within a unified learned architecture.

A world model can become an important component within such a foundation model. Instead of mapping observations directly to actions, a general robot model may maintain an internal state, predict future representations, estimate action consequences, and use those predictions during reasoning or planning. This introduces predictive structure into a broadly pretrained system and provides a mechanism for connecting general knowledge with physical dynamics.

The reverse relationship is also possible. A world model itself can be trained at foundation-model scale. Rather than learning dynamics for only one robot or environment, large-scale pretraining can combine data from many environments, tasks, sensor configurations, and embodiments. The resulting foundation world model could learn reusable regularities involving geometry, motion, objects, interactions, causality, and physical constraints.

This progression changes the traditional role of world models. Earlier models were often designed for narrow environments with relatively small state spaces and specific control tasks. Modern representation learning allows high-dimensional sensory observations to be encoded into learned latent states, while transformers and other sequence architectures can model long temporal contexts. Large datasets can therefore support increasingly general predictive representations of physical environments.

Data requirements also reveal an important difference. Foundation models generally benefit from scale and diversity because broad transfer requires exposure to many concepts and situations. World models additionally require temporal and interaction structure. Independent images can teach visual concepts, but sequences reveal motion and persistence, while action-observation trajectories reveal how an agent's behavior causes changes in the environment.

For this reason, robot experience is particularly valuable for combining the two paradigms. Camera streams, LiDAR sequences, proprioception, force measurements, language instructions, task outcomes, and control actions can jointly provide semantic, geometric, temporal, and causal information. Large-scale multimodal robot datasets can therefore support both foundation-model pretraining and the acquisition of predictive world dynamics.

Representation objectives can also differ. A foundation model may learn features useful for recognition, language understanding, retrieval, reasoning, or action generation. A world model requires representations that remain useful under temporal prediction. Important properties should persist through the latent state so that future configurations and action consequences can be estimated. Prediction therefore acts as an additional constraint on what information the representation must preserve.

This helps explain the relevance of latent predictive architectures. A physical system does not necessarily need to generate every future pixel in order to understand what may happen. Instead, it can predict task-relevant features in representation space. When combined with large-scale pretraining, such approaches offer a possible path toward foundation models that possess broad semantic knowledge while maintaining efficient predictive representations of physical dynamics.

Generalization remains a central objective of both approaches, although it appears in different forms. Foundation models seek transfer across downstream tasks and domains, whereas world models seek predictive validity across changing states and environments. For Physical AI, the stronger objective is to achieve both simultaneously: reusable representations that transfer broadly while continuing to predict physically meaningful consequences under unfamiliar conditions.

Cross-embodiment learning makes this challenge especially significant. A wheeled robot, quadruped, humanoid, manipulator, and aerial vehicle have different action spaces and physical constraints, yet they interact with the same underlying world. A sufficiently general world representation may encode shared environmental structure while embodiment-specific components describe how each platform can act within that structure.

Task heads provide one practical architecture for this separation. A large pretrained representation can serve as a common backbone, while specialized heads perform occupancy prediction, object tracking, navigation, manipulation, value estimation, or control. World-model dynamics can operate on shared latent states, while embodiment-specific action models translate platform capabilities into predicted transitions and executable behaviors.

Language can further extend this architecture by providing semantic abstraction and task context. A robot may understand an instruction such as moving a fragile object to a safe location, but language alone does not determine whether a particular grasp will remain stable or whether a path is physically traversable. A foundation model can contribute semantic reasoning while a world model grounds candidate decisions in predicted physical consequences.

The integration of world models and foundation models also provides a potential route toward stronger physical reasoning. General knowledge can suggest possible strategies, objects, relationships, and goals, while predictive dynamics can test whether those possibilities are compatible with the current environment. The agent can therefore combine knowledge about what might conceptually work with simulation of what is likely to happen physically.

Such integration can support planning architectures in which a foundation model proposes goals, subgoals, or candidate actions and a world model evaluates their predicted outcomes. Unsafe, unreachable, or ineffective possibilities can be rejected before execution. Conversely, world-model predictions can provide structured context back to the reasoning model, allowing higher-level decisions to adapt to changing physical conditions.

The computational requirements of the two systems must nevertheless be considered carefully. Very large foundation models can be expensive to execute continuously on edge robots, while predictive world models may require frequent updates at real-time rates. Physical AI architectures may therefore separate slower high-level semantic reasoning from faster world-state estimation, prediction, planning, and control, while allowing information to flow between these levels.

Ultimately, world models and foundation models should not be treated as competing alternatives. Foundation models provide scale, transferable representations, broad knowledge, and reusable capabilities, while world models provide temporal structure, environmental dynamics, action consequences, and predictive grounding. Their strengths address different requirements of intelligent physical systems and become particularly powerful when integrated within a common architecture.

A long-term direction for Physical AI is therefore the development of foundation-scale world models that combine broad multimodal knowledge with predictive understanding of physical environments. Such systems would learn not only what objects and situations mean, but how they change, interact, and respond to action. This convergence could provide a foundation for robots that perceive, predict, reason, plan, and act across increasingly diverse tasks, environments, and embodiments.

## 00.06. Explicit vs Latent World Models

![](images/image7.png){width="7.268055555555556in" height="7.268055555555556in"}

A world model must represent enough information about an environment to support prediction, planning, and action, but that information can be organized in fundamentally different ways. Explicit world models describe relevant properties through interpretable variables and structures, whereas latent world models encode them within learned representations. Both approaches provide important capabilities for Physical AI and can also be combined within hybrid architectures.

An explicit world model represents environmental state using variables whose meanings are directly defined or interpretable. These may include object position, orientation, velocity, dimensions, semantic class, occupancy, free space, terrain properties, robot pose, or contact state. Because these quantities correspond to recognizable physical concepts, engineers and downstream algorithms can directly inspect, constrain, modify, and reason about them.

Traditional robotics has relied heavily on explicit representations. Localization systems estimate robot poses, mapping systems construct geometric maps, tracking systems maintain object states, and motion planners operate on obstacles and traversable regions. Occupancy grids, point-cloud maps, object lists, scene graphs, kinematic states, and bird's-eye-view representations can all serve as explicit components of a physical world representation.

The major advantage of explicit representations is interpretability. If a planner rejects a trajectory because an occupancy cell contains an obstacle or because a predicted object trajectory intersects the robot path, the reasoning can be examined relatively directly. Explicit variables can also be connected naturally to geometric algorithms, physical constraints, safety rules, control theory, and established engineering knowledge.

Explicit world models are particularly useful when important state variables are known in advance. Navigation may require pose, free space, obstacle geometry, and traversability, while manipulation may require object pose, shape, contact, and gripper configuration. By defining these quantities directly, designers can construct representations closely aligned with the physical variables required by planning and control.

However, explicit representations require decisions about what the state should contain. Complex physical environments include enormous numbers of potentially relevant variables, interactions, and hidden properties. Hand-designed states may omit information that later becomes important or may require increasingly complicated structures as task diversity grows. The difficulty becomes greater when perception must infer abstract relationships that are hard to specify manually.

Latent world models address this limitation by learning internal representations directly from data. An encoder transforms high-dimensional observations such as images, point clouds, proprioceptive measurements, or multimodal sensor streams into compact latent states. These states do not necessarily correspond one-to-one with human-defined physical variables, but they can preserve statistical and structural information useful for prediction, reconstruction, planning, or control.

Instead of predicting every future observation directly, a latent dynamics model can predict how the latent state changes through time. The current latent state, action, and contextual information can be processed by a transition model to estimate a future latent state. Multi-step rollouts can then generate sequences of predicted representations, creating an internal predictive process without reconstructing the complete sensory world at every step.

Latent representations can be computationally attractive because raw physical observations are extremely high dimensional. A collection of multiple cameras, LiDAR scans, radar measurements, and robot states may contain millions of values at every time step. Compressing these observations into a task-relevant latent state can reduce the computational burden of temporal prediction and allow planning to operate within a more compact representation space.

Another advantage is representational flexibility. Learned latent states can encode combinations of geometry, semantics, motion, interaction patterns, and physical properties without requiring each factor to be manually defined. With sufficiently diverse training data and appropriate objectives, a latent model may discover predictive structure that is difficult to capture using fixed engineered variables, particularly in complex and visually rich environments.

The main limitation is that latent states are often difficult to interpret. A dimension or feature within a learned representation may not correspond to a recognizable physical quantity. This can complicate debugging, validation, safety analysis, and the enforcement of hard constraints. A model may also preserve correlations useful on training data without learning the physical structure needed for reliable behavior under unfamiliar conditions.

Representation collapse provides another challenge for latent modeling. If the training objective does not require sufficiently informative states, the encoder may learn representations that discard important distinctions or converge toward uninformative features. Regularization, contrastive objectives, reconstruction, predictive losses, architectural constraints, and carefully designed target representations can help ensure that the latent state retains useful information about the evolving environment.

Prediction targets also influence what latent representations learn. Pixel reconstruction encourages preservation of visual detail, while future-feature prediction emphasizes information useful for temporal consistency. Action-conditioned prediction encourages the state to preserve information about controllable dynamics and interaction consequences. The training objective therefore helps determine which aspects of the physical world become encoded within the latent representation.

Explicit and latent world models also differ in how they interact with uncertainty. Explicit models can attach probability distributions, covariance estimates, confidence values, or occupancy probabilities to interpretable variables. Latent models can represent uncertainty through stochastic latent variables, probabilistic transition distributions, ensembles, or multiple predicted futures. In both cases, uncertainty becomes essential when observations are incomplete or environmental evolution is ambiguous.

The distinction between explicit and latent models should not be treated as a strict binary choice. Many practical Physical AI systems already combine both forms. Camera and LiDAR encoders may produce learned features that are projected into an explicit BEV representation. Neural networks may estimate occupancy, objects, or traversability, after which conventional planning algorithms operate on those interpretable outputs.

A hybrid world model can preserve explicit structures for quantities that require safety, geometry, or physical constraints while using latent representations for complex patterns that are difficult to engineer manually. Robot pose, collision geometry, and kinematic limits may remain explicit, while semantic context, interaction patterns, or uncertain environmental dynamics are represented through learned latent features.

Object-centric models provide an interesting intermediate approach. Objects can be represented explicitly as persistent entities while their individual attributes or interaction states are encoded by learned embeddings. This preserves compositional structure and identity while allowing neural representations to capture properties that cannot easily be reduced to a small collection of predefined variables.

BEV and occupancy representations can similarly bridge the two paradigms. Their spatial organization is explicit because locations correspond to positions in the physical environment, yet each cell or voxel may contain learned latent features rather than only predefined semantic labels. Such structured latent representations retain geometric grounding while allowing rich information to be learned from multimodal sensory data.

The appropriate representation also depends on the stage of the autonomy stack. Fast safety-critical control may benefit from compact explicit variables with clearly defined physical meaning, while higher-level prediction and reasoning may benefit from richer latent states. Hierarchical architectures can therefore maintain several representations simultaneously, allowing information to move between interpretable physical states and learned abstract representations.

Evaluation must reflect these differences. An explicit world model can be assessed using errors in position, velocity, occupancy, semantics, or other defined quantities. A latent model cannot always be evaluated dimension by dimension, so its representation may instead be judged by predictive accuracy, downstream task performance, planning utility, linear probing, temporal consistency, or the ability to preserve information required for control.

For Physical AI, the central question is therefore not whether explicit or latent representations are universally superior. The important question is which information must remain interpretable and physically grounded, which information can be efficiently learned, and how the representation supports prediction and action. Different robots, environments, computational budgets, and safety requirements will lead to different balances between the two approaches.

The long-term direction is likely to involve increasingly structured hybrid world models. Explicit geometry, physical constraints, object identity, and safety-critical states can provide grounding, while learned latent representations capture complex semantics, interactions, and predictive dynamics. By connecting these levels, Physical AI systems can gain both engineering interpretability and the representational flexibility needed to operate across diverse real-world environments.

## 00.07. Deterministic vs Probabilistic World Models

![](images/image8.png){width="7.268055555555556in" height="7.268055555555556in"}

A deterministic world model assumes that a given state and action lead to one specific predicted future state. If the same conditions are provided repeatedly, the model produces the same transition. This formulation is conceptually simple and computationally attractive, but physical environments frequently contain uncertainty, hidden variables, sensor noise, and multiple plausible outcomes that cannot be represented adequately by a single deterministic prediction.

In mathematical terms, deterministic dynamics can be expressed as a transition from the current state and action directly to the next state. The model learns a function that maps the present representation and selected action into one future representation. This structure is useful when system dynamics are sufficiently predictable, observations are reliable, and the consequences of actions vary little under similar operating conditions.

Many classical robotics models are largely deterministic. Kinematic equations predict robot motion from commanded velocities, rigid-body models estimate physical transitions from forces and torques, and trajectory planners propagate states according to predefined dynamics. When the environment is structured and model parameters are accurately known, deterministic prediction can provide fast, interpretable, and highly effective support for planning and control.

Deterministic models are particularly attractive for real-time Physical AI because they normally require only one predicted trajectory for each candidate action. This reduces computational complexity and makes integration with optimization, model predictive control, and trajectory planning relatively straightforward. For short prediction horizons and well-observed environments, the resulting approximation may be sufficiently accurate for reliable autonomous behavior.

However, determinism in the model does not imply determinism in the physical world. Two apparently identical situations may evolve differently because important variables are hidden or imperfectly measured. Tire friction can vary across terrain, objects may have unknown mass distributions, people may change direction unexpectedly, and manipulation outcomes may depend on contact properties that sensors cannot observe directly.

A deterministic predictor confronted with several plausible futures often tends to produce a representative or average prediction. This can become problematic when the average itself is physically unrealistic. If a pedestrian could move either left or right, predicting a trajectory between those possibilities may describe behavior that never occurs. Multimodal futures therefore expose a fundamental limitation of single-output prediction.

Probabilistic world models address this problem by representing future states as probability distributions rather than single fixed outcomes. Given the current state and action, the model estimates a distribution over possible next states or trajectories. This allows the internal representation to express both what is likely to happen and how uncertain the prediction is, providing richer information for decision making.

Uncertainty can arise from several sources. Aleatoric uncertainty reflects inherent variability or randomness in observations and environmental behavior, while epistemic uncertainty reflects limitations in the model's knowledge. A robot may face aleatoric uncertainty when predicting human motion and epistemic uncertainty when encountering an unfamiliar surface or object that differs significantly from its training experience.

Partial observability creates another major reason for probabilistic modeling. The robot cannot directly observe every variable influencing future dynamics. An object behind an obstacle may occupy several possible locations, or a vehicle hidden at an intersection may or may not be present. A belief state can represent probabilities over these hidden conditions and update them as new evidence becomes available.

Probabilistic world models can represent uncertainty through several mechanisms. They may predict parameters of probability distributions, maintain stochastic latent variables, generate ensembles of possible futures, use mixture models, or sample alternative trajectories. Modern generative approaches can also represent complex multimodal distributions when the number and structure of plausible futures cannot be captured adequately by simple Gaussian assumptions.

Stochastic latent dynamics are especially useful for learned world models. Instead of forcing all future variation into a deterministic latent transition, the model introduces latent random variables that represent uncertain factors influencing state evolution. Sampling different latent variables can produce different plausible futures from the same current condition, allowing internal simulation to explore alternative ways in which the environment may evolve.

Probabilistic prediction becomes valuable when planning under uncertainty. Rather than optimizing a trajectory against only one predicted future, the planner can evaluate candidate actions across multiple possible outcomes. An action may perform well in the most likely scenario but create severe consequences in a less likely one. Risk-aware planning can therefore consider expected performance together with uncertainty and potential failure severity.

This distinction is particularly important for safety-critical Physical AI. Autonomous vehicles, mobile robots, manipulators, and human-interactive systems cannot assume that the most likely prediction will always occur. Maintaining several plausible futures allows the system to preserve safety margins, reduce speed, gather additional information, or select actions that remain acceptable even when environmental evolution differs from the dominant prediction.

Probabilistic models also enable active information gathering. If uncertainty about the world affects a decision, the robot may choose an action partly because it reduces that uncertainty. A mobile robot can change viewpoint to inspect an occluded region, while a manipulator can make exploratory contact to estimate object properties. Action can therefore serve both task execution and improvement of the internal world estimate.

The advantage of probabilistic modeling comes with additional computational cost. Representing distributions, generating multiple samples, maintaining ensembles, or evaluating several future branches can require considerably more computation than producing a single trajectory. This creates an important tradeoff for edge Physical AI systems, where prediction must often operate under strict latency, memory, power, and thermal constraints.

Prediction horizon further amplifies the problem because uncertainty generally grows as predictions extend further into the future. Small errors in the current state or dynamics can accumulate across repeated transitions, producing increasingly diverse possible outcomes. Long-horizon probabilistic prediction may therefore require hierarchical abstractions, uncertainty propagation, selective branching, or periodic replanning rather than exhaustive simulation of every possible future.

Deterministic and probabilistic approaches should not be viewed as mutually exclusive. A world model can use deterministic transitions for well-understood robot dynamics while representing uncertain human behavior or terrain interactions probabilistically. Similarly, the central prediction may remain deterministic while uncertainty estimates or alternative trajectories are generated only for states where ambiguity becomes important.

Hybrid models can allocate probabilistic reasoning according to decision relevance. Stable free-space motion may require little uncertainty modeling, whereas occluded intersections, dynamic crowds, unstable grasps, or unfamiliar terrain may trigger richer probabilistic prediction. Such adaptive computation allows the system to conserve resources during predictable situations while increasing reasoning effort when uncertainty can materially change the selected action.

Training objectives must reflect the desired treatment of uncertainty. Standard regression losses often encourage models toward conditional averages, while probabilistic likelihood objectives allow predictions to represent distributions. Mixture-density methods, variational objectives, ensemble training, diffusion-based prediction, and other generative techniques can capture increasingly complex future distributions when multiple outcomes are physically plausible.

Evaluation must also extend beyond average prediction error. A probabilistic world model should be assessed for calibration, uncertainty quality, coverage of plausible futures, likelihood of observed outcomes, and usefulness for downstream decisions. A model that predicts slightly less accurate mean trajectories but correctly identifies dangerous uncertainty may be more valuable for Physical AI than a model with lower average error but excessive confidence.

The choice between deterministic and probabilistic modeling ultimately depends on environment structure, prediction horizon, computational resources, and the consequences of uncertainty. Deterministic models provide efficiency and simplicity when dynamics are predictable, while probabilistic models provide richer representations when hidden states, ambiguity, stochastic behavior, and multimodal futures materially influence decisions.

Advanced Physical AI will likely combine both approaches within uncertainty-aware predictive architectures. Deterministic computation can handle stable and well-modeled dynamics efficiently, while probabilistic reasoning can focus on uncertain and safety-critical parts of the environment. The objective is not to predict every possible future equally, but to represent enough uncertainty for the robot to choose actions that remain effective as reality departs from expectation.

## 00.08. Roadmap of World Models for Physical AI

![](images/image9.png){width="7.268055555555556in" height="7.268055555555556in"}

The roadmap of world models for Physical AI can be understood as a progression from reactive perception toward predictive, adaptive, and increasingly general physical intelligence. Early robotic systems primarily estimate the present and execute predefined control policies, whereas advanced systems maintain internal representations that anticipate future states, evaluate alternative actions, and continuously update their understanding through interaction with the physical world.

The first stage of this progression is reliable perception and state estimation. Robots must transform raw measurements from cameras, LiDAR, radar, IMUs, proprioception, force sensors, and positioning systems into coherent estimates of themselves and their surroundings. Calibration, synchronization, localization, object detection, tracking, occupancy estimation, and sensor fusion provide the observational foundation upon which more advanced world representations can be constructed.

The next stage introduces persistent internal world representations. Instead of processing each sensor frame independently, the system integrates information across time and maintains estimates of objects, free space, geometry, semantics, robot state, and environmental conditions. Memory becomes essential because physical environments are partially observable, and relevant entities may remain important even when they temporarily disappear from direct sensor observation.

Temporal modeling then transforms persistent representation into predictive representation. The system learns how environmental states evolve across time, allowing it to estimate motion, state transitions, object persistence, and future configurations. Initially, prediction may focus on short horizons and relatively deterministic dynamics, but progressively richer models can extend predictions across multiple timescales and represent interactions among robots, objects, people, and environmental structures.

Action-conditioned prediction represents a major transition toward true world modeling for autonomous agents. The model no longer predicts only how the environment will evolve naturally; it estimates how different robot actions alter future states. Steering, acceleration, grasping, pushing, walking, or manipulating an object can generate different predicted outcomes. This capability creates the internal basis for counterfactual reasoning and model-based planning.

Once action consequences can be predicted, world models can support internal simulation. Candidate behaviors can be rolled forward inside the learned model before they are executed physically. The system can compare alternative trajectories, estimate task success, detect potential collisions, and reject undesirable outcomes. This moves Physical AI beyond direct observation-to-action mappings toward architectures capable of evaluating possible futures before committing to real-world behavior.

Planning becomes increasingly integrated with prediction at the next stage. Model Predictive Control, trajectory optimization, sampling-based planning, model-based reinforcement learning, and latent-space planning can all use predicted future states to select actions. Rather than treating perception, prediction, and planning as isolated modules, advanced architectures can jointly optimize representations according to their usefulness for downstream decisions and physical control.

Uncertainty-aware world modeling forms another essential step in the roadmap. Real environments contain sensor noise, occlusions, unknown properties, unpredictable agents, and multiple plausible futures. Probabilistic representations, belief states, stochastic latent dynamics, ensembles, and multimodal prediction allow systems to express uncertainty explicitly. Planning can then account for both expected outcomes and the risks associated with less likely but potentially dangerous possibilities.

The roadmap also progresses from explicit representations toward increasingly learned and hybrid representations. Classical geometry, maps, object states, and physical constraints remain valuable because they provide interpretability and grounding. Learned latent representations provide flexibility for encoding complex semantics and dynamics. Hybrid models can combine explicit geometry and safety-critical variables with latent predictive features, achieving both engineering reliability and representational richness.

Multimodal world modeling expands this capability by integrating information across sensory and informational modalities. Cameras provide appearance and semantics, LiDAR provides geometry, radar contributes range and velocity, proprioception describes the robot body, and language can provide goals or contextual knowledge. Future world models will increasingly organize these heterogeneous signals into shared representations that support unified reasoning about physical environments.

Scaling data is another major dimension of the roadmap. Small task-specific datasets can support narrow world models, but general Physical AI requires experience across diverse environments, tasks, objects, interactions, and embodiments. Large collections of temporal and action-conditioned robot data are particularly valuable because they contain not only observations but also information about how actions cause physical changes. Simulation and synthetic data can complement expensive real-world experience.

Self-supervised learning will play an increasingly important role as data scales. Most robot experience does not contain detailed human annotations, yet temporal sequences naturally provide supervisory signals. Models can learn by predicting future representations, reconstructing masked observations, aligning sensor modalities, estimating motion, or identifying persistent structures. This allows large quantities of unlabeled physical experience to contribute directly to world-model learning.

Simulation and digital twins can accelerate this development by providing controllable environments for generating experience. Simulators can expose agents to rare events, failures, unusual terrain, and dangerous interactions without corresponding real-world risk. Digital twins can provide more system-specific representations for particular robots or facilities. World models can learn from these environments while real-world data is used to correct the simulation-to-reality gap.

A further stage involves continual and online adaptation. Physical environments change after deployment, and no training dataset can represent every condition a robot will encounter. World models must therefore detect distribution shifts, update uncertain beliefs, incorporate new observations, and adapt without catastrophically forgetting previously learned knowledge. Efficient adaptation is especially important for long-lived autonomous systems operating in open environments.

Hierarchical world models provide a path toward longer prediction horizons. Low-level models can represent fast physical dynamics at high temporal resolution, while higher-level models represent objects, events, tasks, or semantic transitions over longer intervals. Such temporal abstraction reduces the need to simulate every low-level detail when reasoning about distant futures and allows computational resources to be allocated according to the scale of the decision.

Spatial hierarchy is equally important. A robot may require detailed representations of nearby obstacles and contact surfaces while maintaining more abstract information about distant regions. Local world models can support immediate control, regional representations can support navigation, and global representations can maintain persistent environmental knowledge. Hierarchical spatial reasoning can therefore connect reactive safety with long-range planning.

Another important direction is object-centric and interaction-centric world modeling. Instead of representing the environment only as pixels or dense spatial fields, models can maintain persistent entities and relationships. Objects, agents, surfaces, tools, and regions can have identities, attributes, affordances, and interaction histories. Such structured representations may support compositional reasoning and improve generalization when familiar physical relationships appear in unfamiliar configurations.

Cross-embodiment learning represents a more advanced stage of the roadmap. Different robots have different sensors, action spaces, dynamics, and physical constraints, but they operate within shared physical reality. A general world model may separate embodiment-independent environmental structure from embodiment-specific action dynamics. Knowledge learned by manipulators, mobile robots, quadrupeds, humanoids, or autonomous vehicles could then become partially transferable across platforms.

This progression naturally connects world models with foundation models. Large-scale multimodal pretraining can provide semantic knowledge and reusable representations, while world-model objectives add temporal dynamics, physical interaction, and action consequences. Foundation-scale world models could therefore combine broad knowledge with predictive grounding, allowing agents to understand not only what objects and situations mean but also how they are likely to evolve under physical interaction.

At higher levels of capability, world models can support active perception and information-seeking behavior. A robot need not passively accept uncertainty; it can move sensors, change viewpoint, explore an unknown region, or interact with an object to improve its internal estimate. Planning then considers both task progress and information gain, transforming perception itself into an intentional component of intelligent action.

Safety and verification must develop alongside increasing predictive capability. World models used for physical control require calibrated uncertainty, detection of unfamiliar situations, constraint enforcement, fallback behavior, and continuous comparison between predicted and observed outcomes. Safety-critical variables may remain explicitly represented even when much of the environment is encoded in learned latent states, creating architectures in which learning operates within verified physical boundaries.

Computational efficiency will remain a central constraint throughout the roadmap. Physical robots cannot devote unlimited resources to predicting every possible future at maximum resolution. Adaptive computation can reduce prediction effort in stable situations and allocate additional resources when novelty, uncertainty, interaction complexity, or risk increases. Efficient latent representations, hierarchical prediction, selective branching, and edge-cloud cooperation can further extend practical capability.

The long-term objective is not a perfect reconstruction of reality but an internal model that is sufficiently accurate, efficient, general, and uncertainty-aware to support intelligent action. Such a model would continuously integrate perception and memory, predict multiple possible futures, evaluate action consequences, plan across several temporal scales, adapt through experience, and transfer useful physical knowledge across tasks and environments.

The roadmap of world models for Physical AI therefore moves from perception to persistent representation, from representation to prediction, from prediction to action-conditioned simulation, and from simulation to uncertainty-aware planning and continual adaptation. As these capabilities scale across data, modalities, tasks, and embodiments, world models can evolve from specialized predictive components into a central cognitive infrastructure for increasingly general autonomous physical intelligence.
